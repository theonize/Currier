# Leader's Notes — 2 Kings 4:1-7 - Widow's oil multiplied

## Verses

> **1** Now a certain woman of the wives of the sons of the prophets cried out to Elisha, saying, “Your servant my husband is dead. You know that your servant feared Yahweh. Now the creditor has come to take for himself my two children to be slaves.”
>
> **2** Elisha said to her, “What should I do for you? Tell me: what do you have in the house?” She said, “Your servant has nothing in the house, except a pot of oil.”
>
> **3** Then he said, “Go, borrow empty containers from all your neighbors. Don’t borrow just a few containers.
>
> **4** Go in and shut the door on you and on your sons, and pour oil into all those containers; and set aside those which are full.”
>
> **5** So she went from him, and shut the door on herself and on her sons. They brought the containers to her, and she poured oil.
>
> **6** When the containers were full, she said to her son, “Bring me another container.” He said to her, “There isn’t another container.” Then the oil stopped flowing.
>
> **7** Then she came and told the man of God. He said, “Go, sell the oil, and pay your debt; and you and your sons live on the rest.”
>
> — *World English Bible (WEB)*

---

## Overview

- **Setting:** Northern kingdom of Israel, sometime between ca. 850 and 800 BC, most likely early in that window, under Jehoram son of Ahab (ca. 852–841 BC). Baal worship had royal backing; the prophets of Yahweh were a poor, marginal community.
- **Who's who:** Elisha, Elijah's successor (2 Kings 2:9-15). The widow's husband belonged to the **sons of the prophets**, a guild of Yahweh's prophets living near Elisha. They were poor: they borrowed tools and went hungry in famine (2 Kings 4:38; 6:1-7).
- **Author:** An anonymous compiler of Kings, working from stories the prophetic communities preserved. He finished the book in the exile (after 562/561 BC).
- **Place in the book:** First of four "life" miracles in 2 Kings 4: oil for a widow, a son given and raised, a poisoned pot healed, a hundred fed. It comes right after 2 Kings 3, where Elisha told three kings to dig trenches that God then filled with water. Same method, now in one small house.
- **Genre:** Prophetic miracle story told as a command-and-obedience narrative. It is spare, told mostly in dialogue, with a narrator who never says "God multiplied the oil." He just counts the jars.
- **Echo:** It deliberately mirrors Elijah and the widow of Zarephath (1 Kings 17:8-16), marking Elisha as Elijah's heir.
- **Explaining the crisis to students:** When a debt went unpaid, the law let a creditor take the debtor's children as bonded workers. Israel's law limited this to six years and required humane treatment, but it was legal. The creditor isn't breaking the law; the family simply has nothing left. (Historical §4)
- **Big idea:** Yahweh, who has bound himself by covenant to hear the widow's cry, keeps that promise through his prophet's word, turning a faithful household's last flask of oil into provision that pays its debt and sustains its life.
- **Supporting movements:**
  - A faithful family in ruin cries out for help (v.1).
  - The prophet asks what she *has*, not only what she lacks (v.2).
  - She and her sons obey. The oil flows until the last jar is full (vv.3-6).
  - The debt is paid and the family lives on what's left (v.7).
- **Guardrail for youth:** This is not a "have enough faith and God gives you stuff" story. The widow ends with a paid debt and a living, not a fortune. She gives nothing to get something (Application §1; Hermeneutic §4).

## Key Verse

> **2** Elisha said to her, “What should I do for you? Tell me: what do you have in the house?” She said, “Your servant has nothing in the house, except a pot of oil.”

- **Why this verse:** The source's Overview, Literary §4 and Hermeneutic §2 all name "nothing... except" as the hinge. Elisha's question turns her from what she lacks to what she holds, and the exception names the one thing God will use (Theological §2).

## Main Points

1. **Cry for Help** (v.1) — A prophet's widow tells Elisha plainly that her God-fearing husband is dead and a creditor is coming to take her two sons as slaves.
   - **Source:** Overview §3; Historical §4; Linguistic §2; Theological §1
2. **What's in the House?** (v.2) — Elisha asks what she has, and she names one small flask of oil.
   - **Source:** Overview §3; Literary §4; Hermeneutic §2; Theological §2
3. **Borrow, Shut, Pour** (vv.3-6) — She and her sons gather many jars, shut the door, and pour. The oil stops only when there are no more jars.
   - **Source:** Literary §2, §5; Linguistic §2, §4; Theological §2
4. **Sell, Pay, Live** (v.7) — The man of God sends her to sell the oil. The debt is paid and the family lives on the rest.
   - **Source:** Theological §2, §4; Hermeneutic §4; Application §1

## Info

### 1. Cry for Help (v.1)

#### v.1
- **cried out** (Hebrew: צָעַק, *tsa'aq*, H6817) is not just crying. It's the word for a formal appeal to someone who can bring justice, as a person would appeal to the king (2 Kings 6:26; 8:3, 5).
- It's the same word in God's law: widows and orphans who "cry" to God, and "I will surely hear their cry" (Exodus 22:22-23). God has promised to hear this cry.
- She goes to Elisha, not the king's court. Under Ahab's dynasty, the prophet was where God's justice was actually available (Historical §4, §6).
- "Your servant **feared Yahweh**": he was faithful and still died poor and in debt. Faithfulness didn't make the family safe from hardship (Theological §1; compare Psalm 73).
- **creditor** (Hebrew: נֹשֶׁה, *nosheh*, H5383): the lender, acting within the law. He never appears on stage and never speaks.
- **Wordplay:** the husband was Elisha's "servant," and the sons are about to become a creditor's "slaves." It's the same Hebrew word, **עֶבֶד** (*'eved*, H5650). A family that served God is about to be sold into service.
- Her plea has three parts: the fact (he's dead), her claim on Elisha (you know he feared Yahweh), and the threat (the creditor has come).
- Jewish tradition (Josephus, the Targum) named the widow as the wife of Obadiah (1 Kings 18:3-4). The text doesn't say so and leaves her unnamed. Mention only if asked.

### 2. What's in the House? (v.2)

#### v.2
- "What should I do for you?" repeats exactly what Elijah asked Elisha before he was taken up (2 Kings 2:9). Elisha now offers someone else what was once offered to him.
- "What do you have in the house?" turns her from what she's missing to what she actually holds.
- "**Nothing... except**": the whole story turns on that word "except." The one thing she dismisses is the thing God will use.
- **pot** (Hebrew: אָסוּךְ, *'asukh*, H610) appears only here in the Bible. It's a small flask of oil for skin and hair, not a storage jar. "Flask" or "small jar" is closer than "pot."
- It's the same pattern God uses elsewhere: "What is that in your hand?" to Moses (Exodus 4:2), and "How many loaves do you have?" in Mark 6:38.
- She calls herself "your servant," using the humble word a woman used before a superior (**שִׁפְחָה**, *shiphchah*, H8198), as Hannah and Ruth did.

### 3. Borrow, Shut, Pour (vv.3-6)

#### v.3
- "Go, borrow" (Hebrew: שָׁאַל, *sha'al*, H7592) literally means "ask." She has to go door to door and let the neighbors see her need, which cost her something in an honor culture (Historical §7).
- "**Don't borrow just a few**" means "don't scrimp." Elisha doesn't give her a number, so how many jars to gather is up to her.
- The neighbors aren't asked to pay the debt, just to lend empty jars. Their jars end up being the measure of the miracle (Hermeneutic §4).

#### v.4
- "**Shut the door**" makes this private, not a show. Elisha isn't even there, so no one can say he rigged it.
- The same shut door shows up twice more in this chapter (4:21, 33). It marks the place where God works out of sight.
- "On you and on your sons": the boys are inside with her. The miracle is a family project.
- "Set aside those which are full" is an instruction given *before* any oil has flowed. She has to act on the word before she sees anything.

#### v.5
- Her obedience repeats his instruction almost word for word ("shut the door on herself and on her sons"). In Hebrew storytelling, that's how you show exact obedience.
- "They brought... she poured" is in continuous Hebrew verbs: they *kept bringing*, she *kept pouring*. The story slows down so we watch it happen.
- No verb for "multiply" or "bless" is used. The narrator just shows the jars filling.
- The sons were about to be taken as payment. Now they carry the jars that will set them free (Literary §6).

#### v.6
- "Bring me another container." "There isn't another container." A kid gets the line that ends the miracle.
- "**the oil stopped**" literally says "the oil *stood*" (Hebrew: עָמַד, *'amad*, H5975). It didn't run dry. It stood still, as if it could have kept going.
- The limit was never God's supply. It was the number of jars (Literary §5).
- Caution: the text doesn't scold her for gathering too few. Read it as encouragement to expect big things from God, not as "your faith sets the size of your blessing" (Theological §4).

### 4. Sell, Pay, Live (v.7)

#### v.7
- She goes back and tells Elisha before selling anything. She stays accountable and doesn't just run with it (Application §2).
- Elisha now becomes "**the man of God**." The title shifts attention from the man to the God behind the miracle.
- The order matters: sell, then **pay** the debt, then **live on the rest**. Honesty comes first. God doesn't cancel the debt by ignoring it; he provides what justice requires, then more.
- "Pay" (Hebrew: שִׁלֵּם, *shillem*, H7999) comes from the same root as *shalom*, wholeness. Paying the debt makes the family whole again.
- "**The rest**" (Hebrew: נוֹתָר, *notar*, H3498) means leftovers. The same word closes the chapter when a hundred men eat and have "some left over" (2 Kings 4:43-44).
- The story opens with the creditor and ends with "your debt," the same Hebrew root. The sons who were about to be taken in v.1 now **live** in v.7.
- It's enough, not a fortune. Contrast Gehazi in the next chapter, who tries to turn God's work into personal wealth (2 Kings 5:20-27).
- Bigger picture, offered as a connection rather than the author's point: Jesus uses debt as a picture of sin (Matthew 6:12; 18:23-35), and God pays the debt we can't (Colossians 2:14) (Theological §4; Hermeneutic §5).

## Discussion

1. Read v.1 again. List everything that has gone wrong for this family. What is she about to lose?
   - **Leader's note:** Husband dead, no income, a creditor with the law on his side, both sons about to be taken as slaves. Point out that her husband "feared Yahweh." Good people still face hard things (Theological §1). Then note what she *does*: she speaks up plainly to someone who can help, and God has promised to hear that cry (Exodus 22:22-23).
2. Elisha could have just fixed the problem. Why do you think he asks, "What do you have in the house?" first?
   - **Leader's note:** Aim for "nothing... except." God starts with what she already has, however small (Exodus 4:2; Mark 6:38). He treats her as someone who can act, not as someone to be pitied (Hermeneutic §4). Invite students to name "small" things they tend to write off.
3. The oil stopped when the jars ran out (v.6). What was the limit: God's supply, or something else? What does that say about God?
   - **Leader's note:** The jars were the limit, not the oil. "The oil stood," as if it could have kept going. Keep it as encouragement to expect big things from a generous God (Psalm 81:10; Ephesians 3:20). Head off the formula "more faith = more stuff": she ends with a paid debt and enough to live on, not riches (Application §1).
4. The widow didn't hide her problem, and her sons didn't sit it out; they carried the jars. When you or your family hit a hard spot, who do you go to? And where could you be one of the ones carrying jars or lending them?
   - **Leader's note:** Draw on Application §2: move from silent despair to honestly asking for help, and from spectator to someone who lends a "vessel." The sons are the youth entry point (Theological §2, "Household faith"). Let students name concrete, age-appropriate "vessels": time, a skill, a ride, showing up. Don't press anyone to share private family struggles in the group.
