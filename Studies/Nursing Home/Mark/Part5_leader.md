# Leader's Notes — Mark 1:29-34 - Healings at Simon's house

## Verses

> **29** Immediately, when they had come out of the synagogue, they came into the house of Simon and Andrew, with James and John.
>
> **30** Now Simon’s wife’s mother lay sick with a fever, and immediately they told him about her.
>
> **31** He came and took her by the hand, and raised her up. The fever left her, and she served them.
>
> **32** At evening, when the sun had set, they brought to him all who were sick, and those who were possessed by demons.
>
> **33** All the city was gathered together at the door.
>
> **34** He healed many who were sick with various diseases, and cast out many demons. He didn’t allow the demons to speak, because they knew him.
>
> — *World English Bible (WEB)*

---

## Overview

- **Setting**
  - **When and where:** the first Sabbath in Capernaum, probably about AD 28-30. Capernaum was a lakeside village of black basalt houses on the northwest shore of the Sea of Galilee, under Herod Antipas (4 BC-AD 39).
  - **The day:** Mark 1:21-39 tells one continuous Sabbath and the next dawn: synagogue, then the house at about the midday meal hour, then the door at sunset, then prayer before daylight.
  - **Sickness then:** there was no public healthcare. Physicians were costly and unevenly available.
  - **First readers:** most scholars date Mark to about AD 65-70. Tradition places it in Rome; a minority prefer Syria. Either way the hearers were mostly Gentile believers under pressure, and many met in homes (Romans 16:5).
- **Author**
  - The Gospel is unnamed. Early tradition (Papias, quoted by Eusebius) gives it to Mark, Peter's interpreter.
  - The homely detail here fits that tradition, though the detail alone can't prove it.
- **Context in Mark**
  - The four have just been called (1:16-20), and Jesus has just taught and silenced an unclean spirit in the synagogue (1:21-28).
  - Next, he prays before dawn and declines to remain Capernaum's healer (1:35-39).
- **Genre and frame**
  - A healing story (vv.29-31), then Mark's first summary report (vv.32-34).
  - Two matching panels, house and door: one woman, then the whole town. In both, others bring the need to Jesus, and the sufferer doesn't speak.
  - Mark's version is the shortest and most domestic of the three Gospels.
- **Big idea:** On one Sabbath in Capernaum, the authority Jesus showed in the synagogue enters a fisherman's house and fills its doorway: with a wordless grasp of the hand he raises a fevered woman to serve, heals and delivers the town's afflicted at sunset, and silences the demons who know him.
- **Supporting movements**
  - Jesus comes to a bedridden woman, takes her hand and raises her, and her serving shows the cure is complete (vv.29-31).
  - When the sun sets and the Sabbath closes, the town brings its sick and demonized, and Jesus heals many and casts out many demons (vv.32-34a).
  - Jesus will not let the demons speak "because they knew him" (v.34b).
- **Three guardrails for this room**
  - The passage describes what happened. It is not a guarantee of healing on demand.
  - "Many" (v.34) doesn't mean Jesus turned anyone away, and it should not be used to explain unanswered prayer.
  - "She served them" doesn't assign women a domestic role, and it doesn't demand that the sick prove their recovery by being productive.

## Info

### 1. The hand that raises (vv.29-31)

#### v.29
- **Immediately** (Greek: εὐθύς, *euthys*, G2117) is Mark's signature word, 41 times in the Gospel and 11 in chapter 1. Here it ties the house to the synagogue. It is the same Sabbath.
- **Out of the synagogue, into a house:** the authority shown in public now enters a private home. The Lord of the place of worship is also Lord of the home and the sickroom. The kingdom he announced (1:15) doesn't stay behind when people leave worship.
- **"the house of Simon and Andrew":** one Greek article governs both names, which presents the brothers as joint householders. That fits a modest but not destitute family business.
- **The first house in Mark** belongs to the first disciples called. Houses become a setting for teaching and the new family (2:1, 15; 3:31-35; 7:17; 9:28, 33; 10:10). For readers who met in homes, this would likely have spoken directly.
- **"with James and John":** the four named in 1:16-20 are together again. The sentence is slightly clumsy in the third person. C. H. Turner noticed it reads naturally as "we came... into our house," and Richard Bauckham developed this into a case for Peter's perspective. It is suggestive, not proof.
- **A text note:** some important manuscripts read "he came out... he came." Editors prefer the plural, as the WEB has it. The house, the people and the healing are the same either way.
- **The house itself:** about 30 metres south of Capernaum's synagogue, under a fifth-century octagonal church, excavators found a house block with a room they believe was later used as a gathering place. Christians honored the spot as Peter's home from at least the fourth century. Earlier veneration is disputed, and no inscription proves it was Simon's.

#### v.30
- **"Simon's wife's mother"** (Greek: πενθερά, *penthera*, G3994): this episode is the only Gospel evidence that Simon was married. Paul later confirms that Cephas took a believing wife on his travels (1 Corinthians 9:5).
- **Why was she there?** A widow ordinarily looked to her own sons. Perhaps she had no son able to house her, or Simon had settled in his wife's family home. Both are inferences. The text says only that she was there and the family took responsibility for her.
- **lay sick** (Greek: κατάκειμαι, *katakeimai*, G2621) pictures her bedridden by the fever, not merely resting.
- **fever** (Greek: πυρέσσω, *pyressō*, G4445) means "to burn with fever," from the word for fire. Mark gives no diagnosis. In a world without antibiotics it could be dangerous, and it kept her from the honored role of welcoming guests.
- **Not a punishment:** the Torah lists fever among the covenant curses (Deuteronomy 28:22; Leviticus 26:16), and hearers might catch that. But Mark never presents her fever as a judgment, and the wider Bible resists that idea (John 9:2-3).
- **"they told him"** is literally "they tell him" (Greek: λέγουσιν, *legousin*), a present tense that brings the moment close. Mark doesn't say who spoke: the household, the disciples, or both.
- **Intercession at its simplest:** this is the first request in Mark made for someone else. It is plain and prompt (compare 2:3-5; James 5:14). She doesn't speak in the story. Others speak for her.

#### v.31
- **One main verb:** the Greek is "having come, he raised her, by taking her hand." Everything turns on "he raised her." The subjects then run Jesus, fever, woman.
- **"He came":** he goes to her. It is a personal gesture where a word would have sufficed. Mark records no word at all, while Luke has Jesus rebuke the fever (Luke 4:39).
- **took** (Greek: κρατέω, *krateō*, G2902) is a firm grasp, a gesture of authority and of help. Fever brought no ritual impurity, so this touch broke no purity rule. Later the same verb describes Jesus' arrest (14:46). The hand that grasps to heal will itself be grasped to be bound.
- **raised** (Greek: ἐγείρω, *egeirō*, G1453) is an ordinary word for helping someone up. Yet Mark repeats the grasp and the raising with a dead girl (5:41) and a boy left as if dead (9:27), and the verb returns at the tomb (16:6). The echo is suggestive, but it should not be pressed too far.
- **"The fever left her"** was probably the ordinary way to describe a fever breaking (John 4:52). She moves from lying down, to raised up, to serving.
- **served** (Greek: διακονέω, *diakoneō*, G1247) first means she took up the work of hosting, most likely setting out the Sabbath meal prepared the day before. It shows the cure was complete: no time to recover. The Greek tense suggests she began and kept on.
- **A weighty word:** Mark uses it of the angels (1:13), of the Son of Man (10:45) and of the women at the cross (15:41). She is the first human in Mark to serve. That link is an inference from Mark's vocabulary. Don't make it carry claims about church office.
- **Not a payment:** her service doesn't pay for her healing or put her back "in her place." Fever had removed her from an honored role, and the healing restores it. Preachers such as Jerome made the fever a figure for sin, but Mark gives no signal of that.

### 2. The town at the door (vv.32-34a)

#### v.32
- **"At evening, when the sun had set":** Mark gives the time twice. "Evening" could cover late afternoon, but sunset is a fixed point. The Jewish day ran from evening to evening (Leviticus 23:32), so sunset ended the Sabbath of 1:21.
- **Why did they wait?** Mark doesn't say. The most probable reason is the ban on carrying burdens on the Sabbath (Jeremiah 17:21-22). If so, their waiting was conscientious obedience, not hypocrisy.
- **A quiet contrast:** Jesus had already healed on this Sabbath (v.31). Mark makes nothing of it yet, but the conflict surfaces in 2:23-3:6.
- **they brought** (Greek: ἔφερον, *epheron*, G5342) pictures a steady stream of people. Many of the sick could not have come alone. Neighbors carried them.
- **Two groups:** "all who were sick" and "those who were possessed by demons." Mark keeps them apart and uses different verbs for the cures (v.34). Nothing in his vocabulary traces all illness to demons.
- **Handle with care:** treating every illness, depression or disability as demonic wounds people. So does dismissing spiritual evil as superstition. Both are errors the text corrects.
- **possessed by demons** (Greek: δαιμονίζομαι, *daimonizomai*, G1139) means "demonized," under a demon's influence or control. These are image-bearers whose voices have been taken over.

#### v.33
- **"All the city"** is the city standing for its people, and "all" is a deliberate overstatement. Capernaum was a large village, with modern estimates from several hundred to under two thousand. Picture perhaps a few hundred people filling a narrow lane and courtyard entrance.
- **was gathered together** (Greek: ἐπισυνάγω, *episynagō*, G1996) freezes a scene: the town standing packed in place.
- **A change of gathering-place:** "synagogue" and "gathered together" share a root. Whether or not Mark meant the echo, the crowd now assembles wherever Jesus is. The verb returns at 13:27 for the gathering of the chosen, but any link is tentative.
- **The door** (Greek: θύρα, *thyra*, G2374) is where household meets street. The crowd returns to it at 2:2.
- **A widening circle:** synagogue, house, door, then "all Galilee" (1:38-39). No sphere lies outside his authority.
- **A crowd is not a company of disciples:** the town came for what Jesus could do, and he met them with generous power. But the next morning he slipped away to pray, then left for other towns to preach (1:35-38). Popular demand did not set his agenda.

#### v.34a
- **He healed** (Greek: θεραπεύω, *therapeuō*, G2323) and **cast out** (Greek: ἐκβάλλω, *ekballō*, G1544) sum up the evening. The single scene gives the summary its texture. Imagine each of the many as another hand taken.
- **various** (Greek: ποικίλος, *poikilos*, G4164) means "many-colored." The Greek Old Testament uses it for Joseph's tunic (Genesis 37:3). In 1 Peter 4:10 it describes God's "various" grace, a verbal echo but an apt one.
- **Yahweh who heals:** healing belongs to God (Exodus 15:26; Psalm 103:3). The description echoes the work Israel's Scriptures assign to Israel's God.
- **"many":** some read it as an inclusive Semitic idiom, and Matthew 8:16 and Luke 4:40 report that he healed them all. It stresses how much he did, not who was left out.
- **No technique:** physicians charged fees, exorcists used roots, rings and invoked names (Josephus, *Antiquities* 8.46-49), and Greek healing shrines used temple sleep and offerings. Mark reports no method and no ritual. The authority is his own.
- **Signs, not a guarantee:** the healings show that the kingdom has drawn near (1:15). Matthew ties this evening to the Servant's suffering (Matthew 8:16-17; Isaiah 53:4). They are firstfruits, not the full harvest.
- **Not yet healed:** the New Testament shows God healing in answer to prayer (James 5:14-16). It also shows faithful believers who stayed sick or recovered slowly (2 Corinthians 12:7-9; Philippians 2:25-27; 2 Timothy 4:20). Full healing waits for the resurrection (Romans 8:23; Revelation 21:4).

### 3. The silenced witnesses (v.34b)

#### v.34b
- **"He didn't allow"** (Greek: ἀφίημι, *aphiēmi*, G863) is a standing policy, "he would not permit." It is the same verb as "left" in v.31, though it is a common verb, so don't press the link.
- **knew** (Greek: οἶδα, *oida*, G1492) is real recognition. The synagogue spirit had just used this verb and named him "the Holy One of God" (1:24).
- **A text note:** some manuscripts add that they knew him "to be the Christ," probably borrowed from Luke 4:41. The short text stands, and the teaching doesn't change.
- **Irony:** the only ones in the scene who know who Jesus is are the demons, and they aren't allowed to speak. The crowd doesn't yet know whom it has come to.
- **The "messianic secret":** William Wrede (1901) named this pattern (1:44; 3:11-12; 5:43; 8:30; 9:9) and proposed that it was a later construction. Many interpreters have since argued that the commands are historically plausible and purposeful. This is the first one the narrator reports, and the first time Mark gives a reason.
- **Why silence true words?** Jesus will not be defined by unclean spirits, or by popular hopes for a wonder-worker. His identity can be rightly confessed only in light of the cross and resurrection (9:9). The first human in Mark to call him "Son of God" is the centurion who watches him die (15:39).
- **Mastery, not anxiety:** naming a power could be a bid for leverage over it. Jesus doesn't negotiate. Paul later does the same (Acts 16:16-18).
- **Knowing is not following:** "The demons also believe, and shudder" (James 2:19). The healed woman serves, while the demons know and are silenced. It is the intruding voice that is silenced, not the person. Jesus' aim is to give people back to themselves (5:15).

## Discussion

1. In vv.30-32, who brings the sick to Jesus, and what does Jesus do for Simon's mother-in-law?
   - **Leader's note:** Unnamed people tell him about her (v.30), and neighbors bring the sick (v.32). She doesn't speak. Jesus comes, takes her by the hand and raises her up (v.31). Let the group name each action. He comes close and touches her, treating her as a person to be lifted rather than a problem to be fixed from a distance.
2. The woman was raised and served. The demons knew Jesus and were silenced. What is the difference between knowing about Jesus and following him?
   - **Leader's note:** The demons had accurate facts and no allegiance (James 2:19). Her service is the wiser kind of knowing. Stress that service was her grateful response, not payment for healing. In Mark it is the shape of the Son of Man's own life (10:45). No one here needs to prove anything by being productive.
3. This passage does not promise that every fever will break tonight. What does it show about Jesus that we can trust while we wait?
   - **Leader's note:** Expect honest answers, and let them stand. Jesus really has authority over sickness and evil, and he comes near in person. Others stayed sick or recovered slowly (2 Timothy 4:20; Philippians 2:25-27), and full healing waits for the resurrection (Romans 8:23; Revelation 21:4). Do not use "many" in v.34 to explain unanswered prayer. Never suggest that illness is a punishment, and never shame anyone whose recovery is slower.
4. The woman in bed did not have to speak. Others told Jesus about her. Whose name can you bring to Jesus today, and whose hand could you take?
   - **Leader's note:** Prayer for others is open to everyone in the room, with or without strength. Encourage naming a person simply and soon, as in Simon's house (v.30). Offer a hand only where it is welcome. Some may feel they have nothing to give. They can be told about, be carried, and let someone else take their hand. Accepting help is its own form of humility and faith.
