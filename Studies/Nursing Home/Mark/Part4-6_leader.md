## Overview

- **Setting:** The lesson begins on a Sabbath in Capernaum, a Jewish lakeside community in Galilee, early in Jesus’ public ministry. The sources place the events approximately around AD 28–30, with a slightly wider range in the first study. The synagogue was a place for hearing Scripture explained. Jesus then enters Simon and Andrew’s household, and the town gathers at its door after sunset.
- **Author and audience:** The Gospel does not name its author. Early church tradition identifies Mark and connects his account with Peter’s testimony. The sources favor composition around AD 65–70 for a largely Gentile readership, traditionally located in Rome, while acknowledging other proposals. These background judgments help orient the leader; they are not facts stated by this passage.
- **Immediate context:** Jesus has announced God’s approaching kingdom and called four fishermen (Mark 1:14-20). They now witness his authority in public worship and ordinary domestic life. The evening’s crowds lead into solitary prayer before dawn. The subsequent Galilean tour and cleansing are not all confined to that same day; the sources cannot fix their duration.
- **Genre and frame:** This is Gospel narrative: Jesus’ identity emerges through words and deeds. Teaching and authority frame the synagogue deliverance. A close-up healing becomes a summary of many healings. The final movement joins prayer, proclamation, cleansing, and the consequences of disobedient publicity. These scenes belong together rather than forming three unrelated miracle lessons.
- **Big idea:** Jesus’ authority brings compassionate restoration, while his prayerful mission calls people beyond amazement into trust and obedience.
- **Authority that frees:** Jesus commands without a ritual or borrowed name. The spirit obeys; the astonished crowd still has more to learn about who he is.
- **Mercy that comes near:** Jesus approaches a bedridden woman personally. Her service demonstrates recovery, not payment for mercy. Mark distinguishes sickness from demonic affliction.
- **Mission shaped by prayer:** Jesus will not let popularity determine his purpose. His preaching continues alongside willing compassion for an excluded man. Restoration includes belonging and worship, and grateful excitement must still listen to Jesus.
- **Pastoral emphasis:** The sources explicitly reject guaranteed immediate healing and productivity tests for the sick. Receiving help is a faithful response. Invite residents to listen, pray, or share without pressure; dignified care includes consent and medical precautions.

## Info

### 1. Authority that frees (vv.21-28)

#### v.21
- Capernaum is the setting; the Sabbath supplies the time.
- The synagogue gathered people for Scripture and teaching.
- The disciples accompany Jesus after their call (Mark 1:16-20).
- The public scene begins with teaching, not a search for spirits.

#### v.22
- The hearers are astonished by Jesus’ teaching.
- **Authority** (Greek: ἐξουσία, *exousia*) means rightful power, not mere force.
- Scribes were trained interpreters; their authority was derived.
- Teaching and authority return in the crowd’s verdict (v.27).

#### v.23
- A man with an unclean spirit is inside the synagogue.
- The outcry interrupts the teaching scene.
- Mark gives neither his history nor a diagnosis of ordinary illness.
- Keep the sufferer distinct from the spirit that afflicts him.

#### v.24
- The spirit recognizes Jesus as the Holy One of God.
- Holy and unclean stand in sharp contrast.
- Its fear of destruction signals opposition, not trusting faith (James 2:19).
- The plural “us” probably represents the demonic realm; that is an inference.

#### v.25
- Jesus issues commands to silence and leave.
- **Be quiet** (Greek: φιμόω, *phimoō*) pictures muzzling.
- No object, incantation, or higher name supplies his authority.
- The source locates this scene’s climax here; compare Mark 4:39.

#### v.26
- The spirit convulses the man and cries loudly.
- The violence is attributed to the spirit, not to Jesus.
- Its departure answers Jesus’ command directly.
- This is no diagnostic checklist for disability, seizures, or mental illness.

#### v.27
- The witnesses connect the deed with the teaching.
- Even unclean spirits obey Jesus’ authority.
- Their question reveals wonder, not yet mature understanding.
- The source presses beyond amazement toward faith and listening (Mark 9:7).

#### v.28
- The report spreads throughout Galilee and its surrounding area.
- The scene widens from one building to a region.
- The same “going out” verb links command, departure, and report.
- Growing reputation prepares for crowds; fame is not discipleship.

### 2. Mercy that comes near (vv.29-34)

#### v.29
- Jesus moves directly from synagogue to an ordinary house.
- Simon and Andrew share the home; James and John accompany them.
- These are the four fishermen called in Mark 1:16-20.
- Authority shown publicly reaches domestic life as well.

#### v.30
- Simon’s mother-in-law lies sick with a fever.
- Mark supplies no precise medical diagnosis or blame.
- Others promptly tell Jesus about her; the speakers remain unnamed.
- Application: name needs in prayer, alongside medical care, with permission before sharing details.

#### v.31
- Jesus approaches, takes her hand, and raises her.
- **Raise** (Greek: ἐγείρω, *egeirō*) here means lifting her up; later it also names resurrection.
- The fever leaves; her serving demonstrates restored strength.
- **Serve** (Greek: διακονέω, *diakoneō*) links this scene with Mark 10:45.
- Service follows mercy; it neither earns healing nor establishes her worth.
- Do not demand productivity from sick residents; receiving care is also faithful.

#### v.32
- The double time notice fixes the gathering after sunset.
- The likely reason for waiting is the end of Sabbath carrying restrictions.
- “They brought” pictures continuing arrivals, not an isolated request.
- Mark lists the sick and those afflicted by demons distinctly.

#### v.33
- The town gathers at the household doorway.
- “All the city” conveys the crowd’s scale, not a census.
- The doorway connects private care with public need.
- The crowd prepares for the pressure voiced in v.37.

#### v.34
- Jesus heals various diseases and casts out demons: distinct actions.
- “Many” stresses scale; do not infer failed cures or explain unanswered prayer from it.
- The demons know Jesus, but he refuses their testimony.
- Knowledge about him is not the same as allegiance to him.
- These healings anticipate final restoration; they do not guarantee every illness ends now (Romans 8:23).

### 3. Mission shaped by prayer (vv.35-45)

#### v.35
- After the crowded evening, Jesus withdraws while it is dark.
- A deserted place means uninhabited country, not necessarily sandy desert.
- The Greek tense portrays continuing prayer.
- Application: protect communion with God; the hour and location are not rules for residents.

#### v.36
- Simon leads the search, with companions left unnamed here.
- **Followed after** (Greek: καταδιώκω, *katadiōkō*) conveys determined pursuit.
- The context suggests an interruption of prayer, not hostility.
- The disciples must learn that Jesus’ mission exceeds local demand.

#### v.37
- They find Jesus and report that everyone seeks him.
- The language conveys widespread interest, not a headcount.
- Popular enthusiasm does not by itself demonstrate understanding.
- Jesus’ reply, not the crowd’s demand, determines the next movement.

#### v.38
- Jesus invites the disciples to go with him elsewhere.
- He expressly names preaching as the purpose of moving on.
- **Preach** (Greek: κηρύσσω, *kēryssō*) means public proclamation as a herald.
- “Came out” can refer locally to leaving Capernaum; a wider mission sense is possible.
- Moving on does not mean indifference to sufferers: the next scene shows compassionate action.

#### v.39
- The summary widens one synagogue’s ministry to Galilee.
- Preaching and expelling demons remain joined.
- Words announce God’s reign; deeds demonstrate its authority.
- The tour’s length is unknown; do not force it into one morning.

#### v.40
- The man comes begging and kneeling, expressing confidence that Jesus can cleanse.
- “If you want to” leaves the decision with Jesus; doubt versus deference is debated.
- “Leprosy” covers a broader ancient skin-disease category than modern Hansen’s disease.
- Leviticus 13:45-46 explains exclusion; impurity is not proof of personal guilt.
- The man seeks cleansing that opens the way to community and worship.

#### v.41
- Jesus stretches out his hand and touches the excluded man.
- “I want to” directly answers the man’s petition.
- **Make clean** (Greek: καθαρίζω, *katharizō*) concerns real cleansing, not merely a declaration.
- WEB reads compassion; some witnesses read anger. Neither changes the willing touch or cure.
- The source sees divine authority in Jesus’ own will and effective word.
- Application: come near with consent and medical safeguards; do not promise a particular outcome.

#### v.42
- The disease departs immediately.
- “He was made clean” fulfills “Be made clean.”
- The repeated cleansing vocabulary joins petition, command, and result.
- Jesus accomplishes the cure before the priest is asked to certify it.

#### v.43
- Jesus gives a strict warning and sends the man out immediately.
- **Strictly warned** (Greek: ἐμβριμάομαι, *embrimaomai*) is forceful language.
- The dismissal introduces the instructions that follow.
- Compassion does not make obedience optional.

#### v.44
- Jesus commands silence and directs the man to the priest.
- Leviticus 14 supplies the inspection and offerings commanded through Moses.
- The priest recognizes cleansing; he does not produce it.
- The procedure provides public recognition and a path back to worship.
- “Testimony to them” may also carry a warning; the precise audience is not stated.
- The silence belongs to this mission setting, not a permanent ban on Christian witness.

#### v.45
- Instead, the healed man spreads the report widely.
- “Proclaim” uses the same Greek verb as Jesus’ preaching.
- Publicity keeps Jesus from entering towns openly: enthusiasm has a cost.
- The former outsider can circulate while Jesus remains outside; the source notes the irony.
- People still come from everywhere; the mission is hindered, not extinguished.
- Whether the man visited the priest is not reported; do not invent his later history.

## Discussion

1. What do Jesus’ words and actions reveal about him across these three parts?
   - **Leader’s note:** Trace command and obedience (vv.25-27), personal care (v.31), and willing cleansing (vv.40-42). Keep attention on Jesus, not sensational accounts of spirits. All three sources’ Theological and Application sections connect authority with restoration.

2. Why does Jesus move on, and how does the healed man’s publicity affect his mission?
   - **Leader’s note:** Use vv.35-39 and vv.43-45: prayer and proclamation precede popularity; well-meant publicity can disobey. The third source’s Application §§1–4 keeps proclamation and compassion together. Do not portray needy people as nuisances.

3. How can we bring needs to Jesus and welcome someone who feels alone, while receiving help ourselves?
   - **Leader’s note:** Draw from the second source’s Application §§2–3 and the third’s Application §§3–4. Prayer, a welcome, a visit, or accepting care can be fitting responses. Do not require a public medical disclosure, physical activity, recovery, or touch. The second source’s Application §1 rejects productivity tests and guaranteed cures.

## Gaps in the Source

- **Combined-source adaptation:** The supplied studies separately select vv.25, 31, and 41. This unified lesson retains v.41 as its single key verse, supported by the third source’s Overview §3 and Theological §4 and by the authority-and-restoration themes of the other two. Their smaller movements are grouped into the three requested source ranges, not claimed as an original three-part outline in any one source.
- **Unreported chronology and prayer:** Neither the words of Jesus’ prayer nor the duration of the Galilean tour is supplied. The leper encounter has no fixed date or location in Mark. Do not reconstruct these details.
- **Unreported personal histories:** Mark does not supply the synagogue man’s later response, a medical diagnosis for the fever, or confirmation that the cleansed man went to the priest. The lesson leaves these unanswered.
- **Handout compression deviation:** To retain all three Scripture blocks and their attributions verbatim within 1,200 words, the handout uses one brief bullet per verse and a shorter Info section than the skill’s usual 400–550 words. Every verse heading, the unified overview, key verse, identical main points, and identical discussion questions remain.
