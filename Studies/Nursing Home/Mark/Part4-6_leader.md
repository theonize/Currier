# Leader's Notes — Mark 1:21-45 - Authority at work in Galilee

## Overview

- **Setting**
  - **When and where:** about AD 28 in Galilee, under Herod Antipas (4 BC-AD 39), who had just imprisoned John (1:14; 6:17-29). Capernaum was a Jewish fishing and farming village of black basalt houses on the northwest shore of the Sea of Galilee.
  - **The day:** Mark 1:21-39 tells one continuous Sabbath and the next dawn: synagogue, house, door at sunset, then prayer before daylight. Verse 39 compresses a tour of unknown length, and the leper scene (v.40) has no time or place marker.
  - **Sickness then:** there was no public healthcare, and physicians were costly and unevenly available.
  - **First readers:** Mark was most likely written about AD 65-70, probably in Rome, for a pressured church. Some scholars propose Syria or Galilee.
- **Author**
  - The Gospel is unnamed. Early tradition (Papias, quoted by Eusebius) gives it to John Mark, Peter's interpreter. Capernaum was Peter's home (v.29), and the detail here fits that tradition without proving it.
- **Context in Mark**
  - Jesus has announced that God's Kingdom is at hand (1:15) and called four fishermen (1:16-20). Their first experience of following him is watching his authority at work.
  - Verse 39 sums up the Capernaum sequence, "preaching and casting out demons," which makes the synagogue scene the pattern of his ministry.
  - The cleansing brings in the priest and Moses for the first time, and v.45 leads into the controversies of 2:1-3:6.
- **Genre and frame**
  - An exorcism story set inside a teaching scene (vv.21-28), a healing story and Mark's first summary report (vv.29-34), a mission saying and summary (vv.35-39), then a miracle story with a stern charge (vv.40-45).
  - The setting widens: synagogue, house, door, then all Galilee. No sphere lies outside his authority.
  - "Deserted place" (v.35) and "desert places" (v.45) are the same Greek expression. Jesus goes there first by choice and at last by necessity.
- **Big idea:** Jesus' authority moves from synagogue to house to door. Directed by prayer, not popular demand, he cleanses a leper by will, touch and word, ending up outside in the outcast's place.
- **Supporting movements**
  - Jesus teaches, and two commands expel an unclean spirit. (vv.21-28)
  - He raises a fevered woman, then heals many at the door. (vv.29-34)
  - He prays, moves on to preach, and cleanses a leper. (vv.35-45)
- **Guardrails for this room**
  - Keep your eyes on Jesus, not on the demons. Don't copy his method: his bare command is his alone (v.25).
  - Don't confuse illness with the demonic. This text gives no warrant for treating illness of body or mind as demonic by default (vv.32-34). Say this plainly.
  - These scenes describe what happened. They are not a guarantee of healing on demand, and "many" (v.34) should not be used to explain unanswered prayer.
  - Illness is not a sign of guilt (v.40), and "she served them" doesn't demand that the sick prove their recovery by being productive.

## Key Verse

> **41** Being moved with compassion, he stretched out his hand, and touched him, and said to him, “I want to. Be made clean.”

- **Why this verse:** The 1:35-45 research sets it at the meeting of two wills and builds its picture of Christ on its will, touch and word (Overview §3; Literary §2; Theological §4), and it says this cleansing ends the chapter's display of authority (Overview §2). It also gathers the earlier scenes: a word without touch (v.25), a hand without a recorded word (v.31), and now hand and word together. The emotion word is disputed, but the touch and the word are not.

## Info

### 1. The word that expels (vv.21-28)

#### v.21
- **"They went":** the plural implies the four fishermen came along (compare v.29). They are silent witnesses here. Later they receive this same authority (3:15; 6:7).
- **Capernaum** (Greek: Καφαρναούμ, *Kapharnaoum*, G2584) comes from the Hebrew for "village of Nahum." Its houses were rough black basalt, grouped around shared courtyards.
- **synagogue** (Greek: συναγωγή, *synagōgē*, G4864) means a gathering. Philo and Josephus describe the Sabbath meeting mainly as a time to read and explain the Law. A qualified man, sometimes a visitor, could be invited to speak (Luke 4:16-21; Acts 13:14-15). That explains how a teacher with no office could teach here.
- **"taught":** Mark doesn't report what Jesus said, only how it struck them. Jesus' first public teaching in Mark is told by its effect.

#### v.22
- **astonished** (Greek: ἐκπλήσσω, *ekplēssō*, G1605) literally means "struck out" of one's composure. "Dumbstruck" captures the image. The tense pictures astonishment that kept going.
- **authority** (Greek: ἐξουσία, *exousia*, G1849) means rightful authority, the freedom to act that belongs to a legitimate office. It is not raw strength. The word appears ten times in Mark, and these two uses (vv.22, 27) come first. Daniel 7:14 uses it in the Greek versions for the dominion given to one like a son of man.
- **scribes** (Greek: γραμματεύς, *grammateus*, G1122) were literate experts who copied, interpreted and applied the Law. Their authority was real but derived, resting on the text and on accepted interpretation.
- **Beyond the prophets:** the prophets introduced God's word with "Yahweh says" (Amos 1:3). Jesus uses no such preface, though he had no formal training (6:2-3; John 7:15).
- **Not an attack on study:** Ben Sira's ideal scribe "seeks out the wisdom of all the ancients" (Sirach 39:1). The contrast doesn't condemn that labor. One scribe is later told, "You are not far from God's Kingdom" (12:34).

#### v.23
- **"Immediately"** marks the intrusion. Jesus does not go looking for the spirit. His presence forces the confrontation.
- **unclean spirit** (Greek: πνεῦμα ἀκάθαρτον, *pneuma akatharton*, G4151, G169) joins spirit language to Israel's purity system. "Unclean" meant unfit for God's presence (Leviticus 10:10).
- **An end-time echo:** only one Old Testament text joins the two words. In "that day" God will cause "the spirit of impurity to pass out of the land" (Zechariah 13:2). Its Greek is exactly Mark's term. Hearers who knew Scripture might have sensed that day arriving.
- **The man:** he has no name and no words of his own. His condition likely threatened his place in family and community. Mark doesn't say whether anyone knew of it.
- **Not a diagnosis:** Mark distinguishes the sick from the demonized (vv.32-34). The passage presents a spiritual affliction, not a misdiagnosed illness, and gives no warrant for calling illness demonic by default.

#### v.24
- **"What do we have to do with you"** (Greek: Τί ἡμῖν καὶ σοί, *ti hēmin kai soi*) is a Semitic idiom of protest: "Why are you interfering with us?" Compare the widow to Elijah: "You have come to me... to kill my son!" (1 Kings 17:18).
- **"destroy"** (Greek: ἀπόλλυμι, *apollymi*, G622) means destroy utterly. Many Jews expected God's reign to end the spirits' power (Testament of Moses 10:1, not Scripture). Greek manuscripts have no question marks, so it may also be read as an accusation: "You have come to destroy us!"
- **A word that returns:** the Pharisees plot "how they might destroy him" (3:6), and so do the chief priests and scribes (11:18). This irony is drawn from Mark's repeated vocabulary, not from any comment of his.
- **"the Holy One of God"** (Greek: ὁ ἅγιος τοῦ θεοῦ, *ho hagios tou theou*) is rare (elsewhere John 6:69; Luke 4:34). It means one wholly set apart to God, the opposite of "unclean." The phrase alone shouldn't be pressed to claim deity. That claim rests on what Jesus does in the scene. A possible play on "Nazirite" (Judges 13:7) should be held lightly.
- **True words, no trust:** the spirit is more accurate than the crowd, yet its confession is a cry of fear. "The demons also believe, and shudder" (James 2:19).

#### v.25
- **rebuked** (Greek: ἐπιτιμάω, *epitimaō*, G2008) is how the Greek Old Testament renders God's own **rebuke** (Hebrew: גָּעַר, *ga'ar*, H1605) of hostile powers. He "rebuked the Red Sea also, and it was dried up" (Psalm 106:9). Howard Clark Kee argued it had become a word for mastering evil spirits. Here it means mastery, not moral correction.
- **Others defer:** the angel says, "Yahweh rebuke you, Satan!" (Zechariah 3:2). Michael says, "May the Lord rebuke you!" (Jude 9). Jesus simply rebukes. The passage does not state that he is divine. It shows him doing what Scripture reserves for God.
- **Be quiet** (Greek: φιμόω, *phimoō*, G5392) literally means "be muzzled," as in "You shall not muzzle the ox" (Deuteronomy 25:4). Mark uses it once more, to the sea (4:39).
- **Few words:** five in Greek, seven in the WEB. These are the only words Jesus speaks in the passage, and the spirit uses far more.
- **What is missing:** Tobit tells of smoke from a fish's heart and liver (Tobit 8:2-3). Josephus tells of Eleazar with a ring and a root (*Antiquities* 8.46-49). Jesus uses no object, no formula, and no higher name.
- **Why silence him?** This is the first of Jesus' commands to silence (later 1:34, 44; 3:12; 8:30). He won't accept testimony from an unclean source, and his identity can't be rightly understood apart from the cross. Compare the centurion at 15:39.
- **His alone:** a bare command in his own name belongs to Jesus. His followers act by his commission and through prayer (3:14-15; 6:7; 9:28-29), never using his name as a spell (Acts 19:13-16). Christians differ on how directly the church should engage such things today.
- **Spirit, not man:** Jesus rebukes the spirit. Mark passes no moral judgment on the sufferer. The captive is not the enemy.

#### v.26
- **convulsing** (Greek: σπαράσσω, *sparassō*, G4682) means to tear or pull to and fro. Mark uses it here and in his last exorcism (9:26).
- **"came out of him"** repeats the command with the same verb, preposition and pronoun (v.25). The word is obeyed exactly.
- **The man afterward:** Mark doesn't describe the man afterward. He drops out the moment he is free, and attention stays on the one who freed him. The Gerasene shows what such freedom restores: "sitting, clothed, and in his right mind" (5:15).
- **An analogy, with care:** breaking with long-held bondage often gets worse before it gets better. That is a pastoral analogy, not a claim that every struggle is demonic.

#### v.27
- **amazed** (Greek: θαμβέομαι, *thambeomai*, G2284) is found in the New Testament only in Mark. "All" is strengthened: absolutely everyone.
- **Wonder is not yet faith:** Mark never says they repented or believed, the response Jesus called for (1:15). Amazement is where wisdom can begin.
- **"A new teaching?"** Mark's first exhibit of Jesus' teaching is a command a spirit obeys. The crowd treats a deed as a teaching.
- **new** (Greek: καινός, *kainos*, G2537) tends to mean new in kind, not merely the latest. That is a tendency, not a rule. God had promised newness: "Behold, I will do a new thing" (Isaiah 43:19).
- **obey** (Greek: ὑπακούω, *hypakouō*, G5219) means "listen under." Mark uses it only here and at 4:41: "Who then is this, that even the wind and the sea obey him?" The question moves from "What?" to "Who?"
- **Forced or free:** the spirits obey by compulsion. Disciples are called to "obedience of faith" (Romans 1:5), doing freely what the demons do unwillingly.

#### v.28
- **report** (Greek: ἀκοή, *akoē*, G189) literally means "hearing," so "what is heard." Isaiah 53:1 uses the same word in the Greek: "Who has believed our report?" Within Mark it is still reputation, not faith.
- **"went out"** (Greek: ἐξέρχομαι, *exerchomai*, G1831) is the verb of vv.25-26. The spirit goes out of the man, and the news goes out into Galilee.
- **Why so fast:** the synagogue was the town's meeting place, and Josephus counts 204 towns and villages in Galilee (*Life* 235). In that dense web a report could travel quickly.
- **Fame has limits:** mere fame can hinder Jesus' mission (v.45). The aim is witness that points to who Jesus is, not only to what is sensational.

### 2. The hand that raises (vv.29-34)

#### v.29
- **Immediately** (Greek: εὐθύς, *euthys*, G2117) is Mark's signature word, 41 times in the Gospel and 11 in chapter 1. Here it ties the house to the synagogue. It is the same Sabbath.
- **Out of the synagogue, into a house:** the authority shown in public now enters a private home. The Lord of the place of worship is also Lord of the home and the sickroom. The kingdom he announced (1:15) doesn't stay behind when people leave worship.
- **The first house in Mark** belongs to the first disciples called. Houses become a setting for teaching and the new family (2:1, 15; 3:31-35; 7:17; 9:28, 33; 10:10). For readers who met in homes, this would likely have spoken directly.
- **The house itself:** about 30 metres south of Capernaum's synagogue, under a fifth-century octagonal church, excavators found a house block with a room they believe was later used as a gathering place. Christians honored the spot as Peter's home from at least the fourth century. Earlier veneration is disputed, and no inscription proves it was Simon's.

#### v.30
- **"Simon's wife's mother"** (Greek: πενθερά, *penthera*, G3994): this episode is the only Gospel evidence that Simon was married. Paul later confirms that Cephas took a believing wife on his travels (1 Corinthians 9:5).
- **fever** (Greek: πυρέσσω, *pyressō*, G4445) means "to burn with fever," from the word for fire. Mark gives no diagnosis. In a world without antibiotics it could be dangerous, and it kept her from the honored role of welcoming guests.
- **Not a punishment:** the Torah lists fever among the covenant curses (Deuteronomy 28:22; Leviticus 26:16), and hearers might catch that. But Mark never presents her fever as a judgment, and the wider Bible resists that idea (John 9:2-3).
- **"they told him"** is literally "they tell him" (Greek: λέγουσιν, *legousin*), a present tense that brings the moment close. Mark doesn't say who spoke: the household, the disciples, or both.
- **Intercession at its simplest:** this is the first request in Mark made for someone else. It is plain and prompt (compare 2:3-5; James 5:14). She doesn't speak in the story. Others speak for her.

#### v.31
- **"He came":** he goes to her. It is a personal gesture where a word would have sufficed. Mark records no word at all, while Luke has Jesus rebuke the fever (Luke 4:39).
- **took** (Greek: κρατέω, *krateō*, G2902) is a firm grasp, a gesture of authority and of help. Fever brought no ritual impurity, so this touch broke no purity rule. Later the same verb describes Jesus' arrest (14:46). The hand that grasps to heal will itself be grasped to be bound.
- **raised** (Greek: ἐγείρω, *egeirō*, G1453) is an ordinary word for helping someone up. Yet Mark repeats the grasp and the raising with a dead girl (5:41) and a boy left as if dead (9:27), and the verb returns at the tomb (16:6). The echo is suggestive, but it should not be pressed too far.
- **served** (Greek: διακονέω, *diakoneō*, G1247) first means she took up the work of hosting, most likely setting out the Sabbath meal prepared the day before. It shows the cure was complete: no time to recover. The Greek tense suggests she began and kept on.
- **A weighty word:** Mark uses it of the angels (1:13), of the Son of Man (10:45) and of the women at the cross (15:41). She is the first human in Mark to serve. That link is an inference from Mark's vocabulary. Don't make it carry claims about church office.
- **Not a payment:** her service doesn't pay for her healing or put her back "in her place." Fever had removed her from an honored role, and the healing restores it. Preachers such as Jerome made the fever a figure for sin, but Mark gives no signal of that.

#### v.32
- **"At evening, when the sun had set":** Mark gives the time twice. "Evening" could cover late afternoon, but sunset is a fixed point. The Jewish day ran from evening to evening (Leviticus 23:32), so sunset ended the Sabbath of v.21.
- **Why did they wait?** Mark doesn't say. The most probable reason is the ban on carrying burdens on the Sabbath (Jeremiah 17:21-22). If so, their waiting was conscientious obedience, not hypocrisy.
- **A quiet contrast:** Jesus had already healed on this Sabbath (v.31). Mark makes nothing of it yet, but the conflict surfaces in 2:23-3:6.
- **they brought** (Greek: ἔφερον, *epheron*, G5342) pictures a steady stream of people. Many of the sick could not have come alone. Neighbors carried them.
- **Two groups:** "all who were sick" and "those who were possessed by demons." Mark keeps them apart and uses different verbs for the cures (v.34). Nothing in his vocabulary traces all illness to demons.
- **Handle with care:** treating every illness, depression or disability as demonic wounds people. So does dismissing spiritual evil as superstition. Both are errors the text corrects.

#### v.33
- **"All the city"** is the city standing for its people, and "all" is a deliberate overstatement. Capernaum was a large village, with modern estimates from several hundred to under two thousand. Picture perhaps a few hundred people filling a narrow lane and courtyard entrance.
- **The door** (Greek: θύρα, *thyra*, G2374) is where household meets street. The crowd returns to it at 2:2.
- **A widening circle:** synagogue, house, door, then "all Galilee" (vv.38-39). No sphere lies outside his authority.
- **A crowd is not a company of disciples:** the town came for what Jesus could do, and he met them with generous power. But the next morning he slipped away to pray, then left for other towns to preach (vv.35-38). Popular demand did not set his agenda.

#### v.34
- **various** (Greek: ποικίλος, *poikilos*, G4164) means "many-colored." The Greek Old Testament uses it for Joseph's tunic (Genesis 37:3). In 1 Peter 4:10 it describes God's "various" grace, a verbal echo but an apt one.
- **Yahweh who heals:** healing belongs to God (Exodus 15:26; Psalm 103:3). The description echoes the work Israel's Scriptures assign to Israel's God.
- **"many":** some read it as an inclusive Semitic idiom, and Matthew 8:16 and Luke 4:40 report that he healed them all. It stresses how much he did, not who was left out.
- **Signs, not a guarantee:** the healings show that the kingdom has drawn near (1:15). Matthew ties this evening to the Servant's suffering (Matthew 8:16-17; Isaiah 53:4). They are firstfruits, not the full harvest.
- **Not yet healed:** the New Testament shows God healing in answer to prayer (James 5:14-16). It also shows faithful believers who stayed sick or recovered slowly (2 Corinthians 12:7-9; Philippians 2:25-27; 2 Timothy 4:20). Full healing waits for the resurrection (Romans 8:23; Revelation 21:4).
- **The "messianic secret":** William Wrede (1901) named this pattern (1:44; 3:11-12; 5:43; 8:30; 9:9) and proposed that it was a later construction. Many interpreters have since argued that the commands are historically plausible and purposeful. This is the first one the narrator reports, and the first time Mark gives a reason.
- **Why silence true words?** Jesus will not be defined by unclean spirits, or by popular hopes for a wonder-worker. His identity can be rightly confessed only in light of the cross and resurrection (9:9). The first human in Mark to call him "Son of God" is the centurion who watches him die (15:39).
- **Knowing is not following:** "The demons also believe, and shudder" (James 2:19). The healed woman serves, while the demons know and are silenced. It is the intruding voice that is silenced, not the person. Jesus' aim is to give people back to themselves (5:15).

### 3. Prayer and the willing touch (vv.35-45)

#### v.35
- **"Early in the morning, while it was still dark":** Mark stacks three time words (Greek: πρωῒ ἔννυχα λίαν, *prōi ennycha lian*, "early, at night, very"). The last Roman night watch, called "early" (13:35), ran roughly from 3:00 to 6:00 a.m.
- **After success, not only in crisis:** the evening before, "All the city was gathered together at the door" (v.33). He withdraws at the high point, before the day's demands begin.
- **deserted place** (Greek: ἔρημος τόπος, *erēmos topos*, G2048) means uninhabited, uncultivated land, not sandy desert. The hills rise steeply behind Capernaum, so a short walk in the dark would reach open country. It is the same word as "the wilderness" of 1:3-4 and 1:12-13.
- **prayed** (Greek: προσηύχετο, *prosēucheto*, from προσεύχομαι, *proseuchomai*, G4336) is an imperfect: "was praying." It pictures prayer that went on for some time. Mark uses this exact form in only one other place, Gethsemane (14:35).
- **Three times in Mark:** Jesus goes off alone to pray here, after feeding the five thousand (6:46), and in Gethsemane (14:32-42). Each is at night and at a turning point. Commentators often note the pattern. Reading it as a deliberate frame is an inference.
- **A word for this room:** the hour is a description, not a rule. The principle is withdrawal and priority, not an alarm setting.

#### v.36
- **"Simon and those who were with him":** Simon is named and the others are not. "Those with him" points back to Andrew, James and John (v.29). This is the first time Mark puts Simon at the head of the group.
- **followed after** (Greek: κατεδίωξεν, *katediōxen*, from καταδιώκω, *katadiōkō*, G2614) occurs only here in the New Testament. The prefix strengthens the verb: "track down, hunt down." The WEB keeps the KJV's mild wording. The NRSVUE has "hunted for him."
- **Intense, not hostile:** in the Greek Old Testament the word mostly describes armed pursuit (Genesis 14:14; Exodus 14:9), but it can describe mercy pursuing the psalmist (Psalm 23:6). Here any sense of intrusion comes from the context: they break in on his prayer.
- **A first, mild misreading:** the disciples see a crowd waiting at the door and assume he should go back. That thread grows sharper later (8:32-33).

#### v.37
- **"Everyone"** (Greek: πάντες, *pantes*) is Mark's sweeping language for the size of a response (1:5, 28, 33). It isn't a census.
- **looking for** (Greek: ζητέω, *zēteō*, G2212) is neutral in itself. But Mark's other uses are mostly negative: his family (3:32), Pharisees seeking a sign (8:11-12), authorities seeking to destroy him (11:18; 12:12; 14:1). Even the faithful women seek him in a tomb he has left (16:6).
- **So don't take it at face value.** The line isn't hostile, and Mark doesn't say the crowd meant harm. He does show that eagerness centered on healings is not yet faith in Jesus' word.
- **Why the town wanted him:** in a village society a powerful healer brought help and honor. The search can reasonably be read as an attempt to keep the healer in Capernaum. That is an inference from social patterns. Mark doesn't spell it out.

#### v.38
- **"Let's go"** (Greek: Ἄγωμεν, *Agōmen*) includes the disciples, even though they came to bring him back. He doesn't scold them. The verb form appears in Mark only here and at Gethsemane: "Rise, let us go" (14:42).
- **Prayer, then decision:** Mark places the prayer of v.35 right before the clear mission of v.38. The decision follows from the prayer.
- **Saying no to a good request:** more healing in Capernaum was a good thing. Jesus doesn't despise healing, as the next scene shows. He won't let popularity set his agenda. "Man is refined by his praise" (Proverbs 27:21).
- **preach** (Greek: κηρύσσω, *kēryssō*, G2784) is the herald's verb: a public announcement on someone else's authority. Mark used it for Jesus' announcement of the Kingdom (1:14-15).
- **"I came out for this reason"** (Greek: ἐξῆλθον, *exēlthon*, G1831) may simply mean he left town that morning (v.35). It may also carry a larger sense of mission. Luke reads it that way: "For this reason I have been sent" (Luke 4:43). Compare 2:17 and 10:45. Mark's wording allows both, and the local sense comes first.

#### v.39
- **One campaign:** "preaching" and "casting out" stand at each end of the verse in Greek. The word announces God's reign. Driving out demons shows that reign at work (v.27). Neither replaces the other, though v.38 names preaching as the stated purpose.
- **What 1:14 announced is now carried out:** Mark echoes that verse almost word for word, now across a whole region.
- **One synagogue becomes many:** the Capernaum pattern of word and exorcism (vv.21-28) widens to "all Galilee." "All Galilee" is a summary of a phase of ministry, not an itinerary.
- **Why synagogues:** visiting teachers could be invited to speak (Luke 4:16-21; Acts 13:15). One gathering could reach most of a village's adults. Jesus works inside the gathered life of Israel, not against it.
- **Keep two groups apart:** Mark distinguishes the sick who are healed from those freed from demons (vv.34, 39). The leper in the next verse is never described as possessed.

#### v.40
- **"A leper"** (Greek: λεπρός, *lepros*, G3015; Hebrew: צָרַעַת, *tsara'at*, H6883) covered a range of visible skin conditions, not only what is now called Hansen's disease. That's why the NRSVUE has "A man with a skin disease." The main concern was impurity, not contagion.
- **What the law required:** torn clothes, loose hair, a covered upper lip, the cry "Unclean! Unclean!" and a home alone "outside of the camp" (Leviticus 13:45-46). These were signs of mourning. In effect he had to mourn for himself, "as one dead" (Numbers 12:12), cut off from home, synagogue and temple.
- **Not a punishment:** leprosy was sometimes a judgment (Numbers 12:10), but Leviticus treats impurity as a condition, not as guilt. Mark gives no hint this man was being punished. Say this plainly to anyone who wonders whether illness is their fault.
- **"came to him":** lepers "stood at a distance" (Luke 17:12), so his approach was socially startling. Earlier, others told Jesus about the sick or brought them (vv.30, 32). This is the first sufferer in Mark to come and plead for himself.
- **"If you want to, you can":** he is certain of the power. The "if" leaves the decision to Jesus. Whether it voices doubt about Jesus' willingness or simply defers to it is debated.
- **What he assumes:** Israel's king said, "Am I God, to kill and to make alive, that this man sends to me to heal a man of his leprosy?" (2 Kings 5:7). This man doesn't ask Jesus to pray. He assumes Jesus himself can do it.
- **make me clean** (Greek: καθαρίζω, *katharizō*, G2511) asks for more than a cure. In that world it meant restoration to the community and to worship.

#### v.41
- **Being moved with compassion** (Greek: σπλαγχνισθείς, *splanchnistheis*, G4697) comes from the word for the inner organs. Compassion is felt in the gut.
- **A text note:** most manuscripts read this, and the WEB follows them. A small group, chiefly Codex Bezae and some Old Latin copies, reads "being angry" (Greek: ὀργισθείς, *orgistheis*, G3710). Scholars are divided, and the NIV (2011) has "indignant." No doctrine changes. If "angry" is right, it can't be at the man, because Jesus touches and cleanses him in the same sentence. Cranfield suggests anger at Satan's disfigurement of God's creature, and France at the presence of such evil in the world. Either way Jesus isn't detached.
- **"stretched out his hand"** (Greek: ἐκτείνω, *ekteinō*, G1614): this is the Old Testament's phrase for God's saving power (Exodus 3:20), and it comes with touch at Jeremiah's call: "Yahweh stretched out his hand, and touched my mouth" (Jeremiah 1:9). Mark doesn't cite these, so don't press them.
- **touched** (Greek: ἅπτομαι, *haptomai*, G680): touching human uncleanness made a person unclean (Leviticus 5:3). Haggai's rule was that impurity spreads and holiness doesn't (Haggai 2:12-13). Here the leprosy gives way. Mark says nothing about Jesus becoming unclean. He reports that the leper becomes clean.
- **Why touch him?** Jesus could have healed with a word, as he did at other times. Calvin says he added his hand "to express the feeling of compassion." His touch reached the man's isolation as surely as his disease.
- **"I want to"** (Greek: Θέλω, *thelō*, G2309) is the man's own verb without the "if." Moses prayed (Numbers 12:13). Elisha sent a messenger (2 Kings 5:10). Jesus says "I want to."
- **"Be made clean"** (Greek: καθαρίσθητι, *katharisthēti*) is a command that does what it says, something the man can't do himself. In Greek the reply is just two words.
- **Shown, not stated:** Mark doesn't state Jesus' divinity here. He shows it, by having Jesus do what Scripture reserves for God.

#### v.42
- **"immediately"** (Greek: εὐθύς, *euthys*, G2117) marks an instant cure. "When he had said this" comes from the WEB's text base. The critical Greek text lacks it, and nothing depends on it.
- **"he was made clean"** closes with the same Greek words as Naaman's cleansing in the Greek Old Testament (2 Kings 5:14). Naaman had a journey and seven washings, and Elisha never touched him. This cleansing is immediate and in person.
- **Only God cleanses lepers:** apart from the sign to Moses (Exodus 4:6-7), the Old Testament records only two cures, Miriam and Naaman. In both, God was the healer.
- **A word of care for this room:** this is not a promise that every illness will be removed. Faithful believers stayed sick (2 Corinthians 12:7-9; 2 Timothy 4:20). Jesus' cleansings are signs that the Kingdom has arrived (Matthew 11:5), and its fullness awaits the day of no more pain (Revelation 21:4). Pray with confidence in his willingness, and don't promise an outcome.
- **Leprosy and sin:** Scripture never calls leprosy itself a picture of sin. Applying "Be made clean" to a guilty conscience (1 John 1:9) is a fair analogy, but it isn't the passage's primary meaning.

#### v.43
- **strictly warned** (Greek: ἐμβριμάομαι, *embrimaomai*, G1690) began as a word for snorting, used of horses. In the New Testament it shows strong feeling expressed outwardly (14:5; Matthew 9:30; John 11:33, 38). All manuscripts have it.
- **sent him out** (Greek: ἐξέβαλεν, *exebalen*, from ἐκβάλλω, *ekballō*, G1544) is the verb of "casting out demons" (v.39). It can mean simply "send out," and that is probably the main sense here. The urgency is: get to the priest, now.
- **Not a gentle farewell:** together the two verbs describe an abrupt, forceful dismissal. "Immediately" now marks the dismissal as it marked the cure.
- **Don't guess at his mood.** Mark seldom reports feelings, so the detail is deliberate. But don't read more into it than is written.

#### v.44
- **The first human told to be silent:** until now only demons have been silenced (vv.25, 34). The series continues (5:43; 7:36; 8:30). Mark 9:9 gives one command a limit, "until after the Son of Man had risen from the dead." Many read the whole series in that light, though some treat commands after healings separately.
- **Why silence?** There were practical reasons, which v.45 shows. Popular hopes also needed correcting. The deepest reason is theological: Jesus can't be rightly understood apart from the cross.
- **Not a rule for us:** the command was specific and time-bound. Jesus sent another man home to tell what the Lord had done (5:19), and the risen Lord commissions open witness (Acts 1:8).
- **"go show yourself to the priest":** the priest confirmed a cleansing that had already happened and did not cause it. So Jesus gives the cleansing and sends the man for the declaration. That verdict was the public certificate that reopened the way to home, synagogue, market and Temple.
- **"the things which Moses commanded":** the steps of Leviticus 14: two birds, cedar, scarlet and hyssop, then washing, shaving and seven days of waiting, then eighth-day offerings. The poor could bring less (Leviticus 14:21-22). For a Galilean that meant a trip to Jerusalem of about three days for a fast traveler (Josephus, *Life* 269).
- **Jesus honors Moses:** he doesn't bypass the Law. The Law could diagnose, exclude and ratify, but it could not make clean (Romans 8:3; Matthew 5:17).
- **"for a testimony to them"** (Greek: εἰς μαρτύριον αὐτοῖς, *eis martyrion autois*, G3142) means evidence, not a speech. "Them" is plural and unclear, most naturally the priests and the public. The same phrase in 6:11 and 13:9 has an edge, so it may also mean evidence against those who refuse it. Mark leaves it open.

#### v.45
- **"But he went out":** this is the only "but" of its kind (Greek: δέ, *de*) in the passage. It falls exactly where the man does the opposite of what he was told. Most take "he" as the man. A minority view makes it Jesus. The Greek never names Jesus in this verse. The WEB supplies the name later in the verse.
- **proclaim** (Greek: κηρύσσειν, *kēryssein*, G2784) is Jesus' own word from vv.38-39. The man told to say nothing is the first person in Mark, after John and Jesus, described as preaching. "The matter" is literally "the word" (Greek: λόγος, *logos*, G3056). Here it most naturally means "the story," and Mark may intend no double meaning, though 2:2 uses it for Jesus' message.
- **Did he go to the priest?** Mark never says. The silence keeps attention on what the man did do. Don't tell the group he skipped it.
- **Grateful and disobedient:** his culture expected public thanks to a benefactor (Seneca, *On Benefits* 2.22-23). His gratitude was probably real. It still blocked the tour Jesus had just set out on. Mark doesn't resolve that tension, and we shouldn't force it. "It isn't good to have zeal without knowledge" (Proverbs 19:2).
- **"could no more":** the same verb as the leper's "you can" (v.40). The one who can cleanse a leper can't walk into a town because of it.
- **Places exchanged:** "outside" (Greek: ἔξω, *exō*, G1854) is the word the Greek Leviticus uses for the leper's dwelling (Leviticus 13:46). The man who lived outside can come back into town, and Jesus ends up "outside in desert places." Mark states this as a consequence, not a doctrine. Many interpreters see a quiet foreshadowing of the cross, where Jesus "suffered outside of the gate" (Hebrews 13:12-13). That is an inference, but the reversal of positions is plainly in the text.
- **"they came to him from everywhere":** "came" is an imperfect, "kept coming." Even "outside," his exclusion doesn't stop the gathering. Human disobedience doesn't defeat his purpose. The verse leads straight into the crowded house of 2:1-2.

## Discussion

1. How does Jesus use his word in v.25, his hand in v.31, and both in v.41?
   - **Leader's note:** Let the group find it. In v.25 he speaks and no one is touched. In v.31 Mark records no word, only a hand. In v.41 he touches the man and speaks. He uses no object, no formula and no higher name in any of them. This is a comparison across the three scenes, so don't turn it into a rule. Let it show how personally he meets each need.
2. The spirit knew Jesus, and the crowds were amazed. Why is neither the same as following him?
   - **Leader's note:** The spirit has true words and no trust: "The demons also believe, and shudder" (James 2:19). Mark never says the crowd repented or believed (v.27; 1:15), and the same astonishment turned to offense at Nazareth (6:2-3). Eagerness for healing is not yet faith (v.37). The woman he raised serves (v.31), though that is a grateful response, not payment. No one here needs to prove anything by being productive.
3. Jesus could have healed the leper with a word. Why do you think he touched him?
   - **Leader's note:** The law kept this man at a distance, and touching him should have made Jesus unclean (Leviticus 5:3). Here the leprosy gives way instead. The touch reached the man's isolation as surely as his disease. Calvin says the hand expressed his compassion. Don't overlook the cost: the passage ends with Jesus "outside" (v.45).
4. Do you believe Jesus can help you, yet wonder if he wants to? Whose name can you bring him today?
   - **Leader's note:** Many believers confess God's power while privately assuming he is reluctant toward them because of their past, their condition or their shame. Point to "I want to" (v.41). Be careful here: this is not a promise of healing (2 Corinthians 12:7-9), illness is not a sign of guilt, and "many" in v.34 must not be used to explain unanswered prayer. Don't suggest that anyone's illness comes from a demon. Prayer for others is open to everyone in the room, with or without strength: the woman in v.30 didn't speak, and others told Jesus about her. Offer to pray with anyone who wishes, and close by praying the leper's honest prayer together (v.40).

## Gaps in the Source

- **No research file treats Mark 1:21-45 as a unit.** There are three files, each with its own big idea and key verse (1:25, 1:31, 1:41). The big idea here joins their wording, and the three main points follow the three files.
- **The key verse is a choice among the three.** The word, hand, and hand-and-word comparison (vv.25, 31, 41) is drawn across the files. None of them states it, so it is offered as an observation, not as Mark's stated pattern.
- **The date differs between files.** The same Sabbath is c. AD 28 (range AD 27-29) in the 1:21-28 file and c. AD 28-30 in the 1:29-34 and 1:35-45 files. "About AD 28" falls within all of them. The handout gives no date.
