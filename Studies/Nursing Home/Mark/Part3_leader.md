# Leader's Notes — Mark 1:14-20 - Jesus begins ministry and calls disciples

## Verses

> **14** Now after John was taken into custody, Jesus came into Galilee, preaching the Good News of God’s Kingdom,
>
> **15** and saying, “The time is fulfilled, and God’s Kingdom is at hand! Repent, and believe in the Good News.”
>
> **16** Passing along by the sea of Galilee, he saw Simon and Andrew the brother of Simon casting a net into the sea, for they were fishermen.
>
> **17** Jesus said to them, “Come after me, and I will make you into fishers for men.”
>
> **18** Immediately they left their nets, and followed him.
>
> **19** Going on a little further from there, he saw James the son of Zebedee, and John, his brother, who were also in the boat mending the nets.
>
> **20** Immediately he called them, and they left their father, Zebedee, in the boat with the hired servants, and went after him.
>
> — *World English Bible (WEB)*

---

## Overview

- **Setting**
  - **When and where:** about AD 27-29, in Galilee. Verses 16-20 most likely take place on the northwest shore of the Sea of Galilee, between Capernaum and the plain of Gennesaret. Tradition points to Tabgha, which is plausible but can't be proven.
  - **Rulers:** Herod Antipas ruled Galilee (4 BC-AD 39). He had just arrested John (Mark 6:17). Josephus confirms that Antipas held John at the fortress of Machaerus and executed him there, fearing an uprising (*Antiquities* 18.116-119).
  - **First readers:** Mark was most likely written about AD 65-70 for mostly Gentile believers, probably in Rome. Some scholars propose Syria. By then James son of Zebedee had been executed (Acts 12:1-2), and Peter had probably died under Nero. They heard this call knowing what following had cost.
- **Author**
  - The Gospel is unnamed. Early tradition (Papias, quoted by Eusebius) gives it to John Mark, Peter's interpreter.
  - Simon is named twice in v.16. He is the first disciple named in Mark and the last (16:7). Richard Bauckham reads this frame as a signal that Peter is Mark's main source. The case is cumulative, not decisive.
- **Context in Mark**
  - The prologue has shown the forerunner, the baptized Son, and his testing (1:1-13). Verses 14-15 are a hinge: "Good News" answers "the beginning of the Good News" (1:1). Scholars debate whether the prologue ends at v.13 or v.15.
  - Next, the four go with Jesus into Capernaum (1:21) and see the Kingdom's first display of power.
  - This launches the first of three Galilean movements. The disciples are called here, appointed in 3:13-19, and sent in 6:7-13.
  - John's Gospel records an earlier meeting of Andrew and Simon with Jesus (John 1:35-42). Mark tells the moment of commitment, not the first meeting. That harmonization is reasonable but not certain.
- **Genre and frame**
  - A summary of Jesus' preaching (vv.14-15), then two matched call scenes (vv.16-18, 19-20).
  - Mark's is the sparest account. He cites no prophecy and reports no miracle (contrast Matthew 4:14-16; Luke 5:1-11), and the fishermen say nothing. Nothing stands between Jesus' word and their obedience.
  - The scenes are parallel, not chiastic: repetition with increase.
- **Big idea:** At the appointed time Jesus announces that God's reign has drawn near and summons all to repent and believe, then calls four fishermen by name, who leave nets and father to follow him.
- **Supporting movements**
  - After John is handed over, Jesus enters Antipas's Galilee announcing the fulfilled time and God's near reign as the ground for repentance and faith (vv.14-15).
  - Jesus turns Simon and Andrew's trade into their calling, and they leave their nets and follow (vv.16-18).
  - The summons shrinks to "he called them" while the cost grows, as James and John leave father, boat and hired servants (vv.19-20).
- **A thread through the whole Gospel:** the first verb, "handed over," is used of John here, then of Jesus (9:31; 15:15), then of the disciples (13:9-12). The call that begins in Galilee is renewed there (16:7).

## Key Verse

> **15** and saying, “The time is fulfilled, and God’s Kingdom is at hand! Repent, and believe in the Good News.”

- **Why this verse:** The Literary section calls it the programmatic thesis that governs the call scenes, the Theological section builds its central claim on it, and the Hermeneutic names it the hinge where promise becomes presence (Overview §3; Literary §1, §4; Theological §3; Hermeneutic §5).

## Main Points

1. **The good news announced** (vv.14-15) — After John is handed over, Jesus comes into Galilee announcing that God's appointed time is full and his reign has drawn near, and he calls everyone to repent and believe.
   - **Source:** Overview §3; Historical §5, §6; Linguistic §2, §3; Theological §1, §3; Hermeneutic §1.
2. **Called from the nets** (vv.16-18) — Jesus sees Simon and Andrew at work and says, "Come after me, and I will make you into fishers for men"; at once they leave their nets and follow.
   - **Source:** Literary §2, §4, §5; Linguistic §2, §4; Historical §4; Theological §2, §4.
3. **Called from the father's boat** (vv.19-20) — Jesus calls James and John at once, and the cost grows: they leave their father, the boat and the hired servants to go after him.
   - **Source:** Literary §2, §4; Historical §4, §5, §7; Theological §2; Application §1.

## Info

### 1. The good news announced (vv.14-15)

#### v.14
- **"taken into custody"** is literally **handed over** (Greek: παραδίδωμι, *paradidōmi*, G3860). No agent is named. Historically it was Herod Antipas (6:17), but the story waits for a flashback (6:17-29).
- **A pattern begins:** Mark uses this verb about twenty times. John preaches and is handed over. Jesus preaches and is handed over (9:31; 15:15). The disciples will preach and be handed over (13:9-12).
- **Antipas's territory:** Galilee belonged to the ruler who had just silenced the most popular preacher in his land. Announcing "God's Kingdom" there was not politically neutral (compare 6:14-16; Luke 13:31).
- **Galilee, not Jerusalem.** The ministry begins in Galilee, and the Gospel ends by sending the disciples back there (14:28; 16:7).
- **preaching** (Greek: κηρύσσω, *kēryssō*, G2784) is a herald's work: announcing a ruler's decree in public, not offering an opinion.
- **Good News** (Greek: εὐαγγέλιον, *euangelion*, G2098) echoes Isaiah's herald: "How beautiful on the mountains are the feet of him who brings good news... who says to Zion, 'Your God reigns!'" (Isaiah 52:7).
- **A rival announcement:** the Priene inscription (9 BC) called Augustus's birthday the beginning of "good tidings" for the world. This good news belongs to God, not Caesar.
- **A text note:** the WEB reads "the Good News of God's Kingdom." Many of the earliest manuscripts read "the Good News of God." The teaching is the same, since v.15 names the content as the Kingdom.

#### v.15
- **Two announcements, then two commands.** "The time is fulfilled" and "God's Kingdom is at hand" come first; "Repent" and "believe" follow. In Greek no "therefore" joins them. What God has done grounds what people must do.
- **time** (Greek: καιρός, *kairos*, G2540) is an appointed, decisive season. **is fulfilled** (Greek: πεπλήρωται, *peplērōtai*, from πληρόω, *plēroō*, G4137) is a perfect passive: a measure filled to the brim. God is the implied one who filled it (Galatians 4:4; compare Daniel 7:22).
- **Kingdom** (Greek: βασιλεία, *basileia*, G932) means God's reign in action more than a territory. The Aramaic paraphrase of Isaiah (the Targum) renders "Your God reigns!" as "the kingdom of your God is revealed."
- **at hand** (Greek: ἐγγίζω, *engizō*, G1448) is debated: "has arrived" (C. H. Dodd) or "has come near." At 14:42 the betrayer "is at hand," and Judas arrives "immediately" (14:43). The Kingdom is on the threshold. Its power is at work in the next scene (1:27), yet its coming "with power" is still ahead (9:1).
- **Repent** (Greek: μετανοέω, *metanoeō*, G3340) carries the prophets' call to **return** (Hebrew: שׁוּב, *shuv*, H7725): "Return to me... and I will return to you" (Zechariah 1:3). It is a turning of the whole life, and it answers grace already offered. It is not a payment to earn the Kingdom.
- **believe in** (Greek: πιστεύω, *pisteuō*, G4100) with "in" is unusual Greek. It most likely reflects the Hebrew "trust in" (Genesis 15:6) and means resting your weight on the news.
- **Together, not apart:** repentance without faith becomes grim self-reform; faith without repentance becomes agreement that changes nothing (Acts 20:21). The present tense suits a lasting posture, but don't press the grammar alone.
- **Not a call to revolt:** some hoped for a Davidic king who would purge Jerusalem of Gentiles (*Psalms of Solomon* 17), and Judas the Galilean led a revolt in AD 6. Jesus calls for repentance and faith, and the first result is four fishermen following him on foot.

### 2. Called from the nets (vv.16-18)

#### v.16
- **"Passing along... he saw":** Jesus is on the move, and he takes the first step. Mark never says the fishermen saw, sought or chose him. The same pattern returns with Levi (2:14).
- **Simon named twice:** "Simon and Andrew the brother of Simon." Mark keeps Simon in the foreground.
- **Names from a bilingual land:** Simon was the most common Jewish male name of the day. Andrew is a purely Greek name.
- **casting a net** (Greek: ἀμφιβάλλω, *amphiballō*) is the technical term for the round, weighted cast-net, thrown by one man from the shore or in shallow water. It is found only here in the New Testament.
- **the sea of Galilee** (Greek: θάλασσα, *thalassa*, G2281) follows Hebrew usage, which calls any large body of water a "sea." Luke calls it a "lake" (Luke 5:1). It is freshwater, about 13 miles long and 8 wide, and about 700 feet below sea level.
- **"for they were fishermen"** is the narrator's only aside in the passage. It sets up Jesus' wordplay in the next verse.
- **A real trade:** K. C. Hanson and Wilhelm Wuellner reconstruct lake fishing as a regulated, taxed business. On that reconstruction these fishermen were small entrepreneurs, not destitute day laborers.

#### v.17
- **Come after me** (Greek: δεῦτε ὀπίσω μου, *deute opisō mou*, G1205, G3694) reflects the Hebrew idiom "walk after" someone, meaning to give that person your allegiance. Israel was told, "You shall walk after Yahweh your God" (Deuteronomy 13:4). Jesus stands where the Torah places God. That is an inference Mark leaves unstated.
- **"After me" again:** John said, "After me comes he who is mightier" (1:7). Now that one says, "Come after me." Later Peter is told, "Get behind me" (8:33-34). Discipleship means walking behind Jesus, not ahead of him.
- **The teacher chooses:** a later Jewish maxim says, "Make for yourself a teacher" (*Mishnah Avot* 1:6). Here the teacher does the choosing (John 15:16), more like a prophet's call (1 Kings 19:19-21).
- **I will make you** (Greek: ποιέω, *poieō*, G4160) **become** (Greek: γίνομαι, *ginomai*, G1096) is a promise, not a job description. The making takes time, and Jesus is the one who does it.
- **fishers for men** (Greek: ἁλιεῖς ἀνθρώπων, *halieis anthrōpōn*, G231, G444) is a pun on their trade. "Men" means human beings of every kind.
- **A reclaimed image:** in the prophets, hooks and nets usually picture judgment (Jeremiah 16:16-18; Amos 4:2; Habakkuk 1:14-17), with one bright exception (Ezekiel 47:10). In Jesus' mouth the image means gathering people for the Kingdom. Luke has "catching people alive" (Luke 5:10).
- **The promise kept:** the Twelve are appointed "to preach" (3:14) and later "preached that people should repent" (6:12). At Pentecost about three thousand were added in a day (Acts 2:41).

#### v.18
- **Immediately** (Greek: εὐθύς, *euthys*, G2117) is Mark's signature word, used about forty times, often just to move the story along. Here the context gives it real urgency.
- **No farewell asked:** Elisha asked to kiss his father and mother goodbye before following Elijah (1 Kings 19:20). These men ask nothing.
- **left** (Greek: ἀφίημι, *aphiēmi*, G863) means "let go, release." The nets stand for a whole livelihood.
- **followed** (Greek: ἀκολουθέω, *akoloutheō*, G190) is the main verb of the sentence. Leaving is what makes following possible.
- **Not impulse:** Mark gives no motive. The story credits their response to the authority of the one calling. If the accounts are harmonized, they may already have known him (John 1:35-42).
- **An echo for re-readers:** Peter later says, "We have left all, and have followed you" (10:28). At the arrest the same verb returns: "They all left him, and fled" (14:50). Yet the risen Jesus sends word to "his disciples and Peter" (16:7).
- **Their only answer is action.** The four never say a word in the passage.

### 3. Called from the father's boat (vv.19-20)

#### v.19
- **"Going on a little further":** the second scene follows the first. Jesus moves, sees two brothers at work, and calls them.
- **The names:** James is the Hebrew Jacob. John means "the LORD has been gracious." Zebedee probably comes from Zabdi or Zebadiah, "the LORD has given."
- **Two Johns:** the passage opens with one John taken from the scene (v.14) and ends with another John drawn into it. A listener notices the effect, though Mark's intent can't be proven.
- **mending** (Greek: καταρτίζω, *katartizō*, G2675) means restoring something to working order. Mendel Nun connects it with the daily repair of nets used from boats in night fishing.
- **The boat:** the "Galilee boat" found in the lakeshore mud in 1986 is about 27 feet long and 7.5 feet wide. It was patched again and again and probably carried a crew of about five. It can't be tied to anyone in the Gospels, but it shows the kind of boat Zebedee worked in.
- **Don't allegorize:** preachers have made "mending" a picture of a ministry of restoration. Mark gives no sign of that meaning.
- **Later in the story:** James becomes one of the inner three (5:37; 9:2; 14:33), and he was the first of these four to die for Jesus (Acts 12:1-2).

#### v.20
- **"Immediately he called them":** this time "immediately" goes with Jesus' call rather than their response. His words aren't repeated, because we already know them. Neither the caller nor the called delays.
- **The cost grows:** the first pair left their nets. This pair leaves nets, boat, father and workforce.
- **A son's duty:** grown sons normally lived in their father's household and took up his trade. Honoring parents included supporting them in old age (Exodus 20:12; Mark 7:9-13). Leaving would have been startling.
- **hired servants** (Greek: μισθωτός, *misthōtos*, G3411) were free wage-laborers, typically paid by the day. Only Mark mentions them. They show a working business of some size, and they suggest that Zebedee wasn't left alone or destitute.
- **went after him** (Greek: ἀπῆλθον ὀπίσω αὐτοῦ, *apēlthon opisō autou*) answers "Come after me" (v.17) with the same word "after."
- **Not cutting every tie:** Simon still had a house in Capernaum (1:29), and the disciples could return to fishing (John 21:3). The call transferred their first allegiance.
- **Not a rule for every believer:** Jesus sent the man freed from a legion of demons home instead: "Go to your house, to your friends, and tell them what great things the Lord has done for you" (5:19). The allegiance is for everyone; the literal departure was for these men.

## Discussion

1. In vv.16-20, what does Jesus do, and what do the four fishermen say or do?
   - **Leader's note:** Jesus passes by, sees, speaks and calls (vv.16-17, 19-20). The fishermen say nothing; they leave and follow. The initiative is his. A later Jewish saying told students to choose a teacher (*Mishnah Avot* 1:6). Here the teacher chooses (John 15:16).
2. In v.15 Jesus announces what God has done before he says what we must do. Why does that order matter?
   - **Leader's note:** The gospel is news before it is demand. "The time is fulfilled" and "God's Kingdom is at hand" ground "Repent" and "believe." Repentance is not a payment to earn the Kingdom; it answers grace already offered. Turning and trusting go together (Acts 20:21).
3. Jesus says, "I will make you into fishers for men." Why is it good news that he does the making?
   - **Leader's note:** It is a promise, not a job description, and the making takes time. These men were not religious professionals (Acts 4:13). The same men later "left him, and fled" (14:50), yet the risen Jesus sent word to "his disciples and Peter" (16:7). The call rests on his faithfulness, not on theirs.
4. Jesus met these men on an ordinary workday and pointed their lives toward other people. Where you are now, who is one person you could pray for by name, or tell what the Lord has done for you?
   - **Leader's note:** Not every disciple is called to leave home or work. Jesus sent one man home to "tell them what great things the Lord has done for you" (5:19). Every follower is still pointed toward other people (v.17). Suggest keeping a short list of a few people to pray for by name. If someone feels they have failed as a follower, point to 16:7: the promise outlasts our failure.
