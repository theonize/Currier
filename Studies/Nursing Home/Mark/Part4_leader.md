# Leader's Notes — Mark 1:21-28 - Authority over demons

## Verses

> **21** They went into Capernaum, and immediately on the Sabbath day he entered into the synagogue and taught.
>
> **22** They were astonished at his teaching, for he taught them as having authority, and not as the scribes.
>
> **23** Immediately there was in their synagogue a man with an unclean spirit, and he cried out,
>
> **24** saying, “Ha! What do we have to do with you, Jesus, you Nazarene? Have you come to destroy us? I know you who you are: the Holy One of God!”
>
> **25** Jesus rebuked him, saying, “Be quiet, and come out of him!”
>
> **26** The unclean spirit, convulsing him and crying with a loud voice, came out of him.
>
> **27** They were all amazed, so that they questioned among themselves, saying, “What is this? A new teaching? For with authority he commands even the unclean spirits, and they obey him!”
>
> **28** The report of him went out immediately everywhere into all the region of Galilee and its surrounding area.
>
> — *World English Bible (WEB)*

---

## Overview

- **Setting**
  - **When and where:** a Sabbath around AD 27-29, most likely about AD 28, in the synagogue at Capernaum, a Jewish fishing and farming village on the northwest shore of the Sea of Galilee of no more than about 1,500 people. It was Simon Peter's home (1:29).
  - **Rulers:** Galilee belonged to Herod Antipas (4 BC-AD 39), who had just imprisoned John the Baptist (1:14; 6:17-29).
  - **First readers:** most scholars date Mark about AD 65-70. Early tradition places it in Rome, and internal evidence points to largely Gentile readers. If Mark wrote in Rome in the late 60s, his readers had survived Nero's persecution, in a city crowded with diviners, amulet-sellers and freelance exorcists. Some scholars place Mark's audience in Syria or Galilee instead; the passage's force doesn't depend on settling it.
- **Author and context**
  - The Gospel is unnamed. Early tradition (Papias, quoted by Eusebius) gives it to John Mark, "Peter's interpreter."
  - This passage opens the "Capernaum day" (1:21-39), where the link to Peter is most visible. Some scholars see eyewitness memory here. That is suggestive, not conclusive.
  - It follows the announcement "God's Kingdom is at hand" (1:15) and the call of four fishermen (1:16-20). "They went into Capernaum": the disciples' first experience of following Jesus is watching his authority at work.
  - The day ends with Jesus going through Galilee "preaching and casting out demons" (1:39). That summary makes this scene the pattern of his ministry.
- **Genre and frame**
  - Jesus' first public act in Mark: an exorcism story set inside a synagogue teaching scene. Teaching and authority frame it (vv.22, 27); the deed is the teaching made visible.
  - It follows the usual shape of ancient exorcism stories but leaves out the usual tools: no object, formula, ritual or higher name.
  - The passage folds around Jesus' command (v.25), his only words. The verbal links are firm; whether Mark consciously built a formal chiasm is less certain.
- **Big idea:** In Jesus' first public act, the authority of his teaching proves itself in a bare command that expels an unclean spirit: God's holy and sovereign authority has arrived in person, and the Kingdom Jesus announced has begun its victory over evil.
- **Supporting movements**
  - Jesus teaches on the Sabbath, and hearers used to the borrowed authority of the scribes are astonished (vv.21-22).
  - An unclean spirit protests, fears destruction, and names him "the Holy One of God" (vv.23-24).
  - Jesus silences it with two commands, and it comes out, convulsing the man (vv.25-26).
  - The crowd calls this a new teaching that even unclean spirits obey, and the report spreads through Galilee (vv.27-28).
- **A word of care for this group:** Mark keeps sickness and evil spirits distinct: "He healed many who were sick with various diseases, and cast out many demons" (1:34). The source presents a spiritual affliction here, not a misdiagnosed illness. It says this text gives no warrant for treating epilepsy, mental illness, or disability as demonic by default. Mark passes no moral judgment on the man; Jesus rebukes the spirit, not the man.

> **25** Jesus rebuked him, saying, “Be quiet, and come out of him!”

## Info

### 1. Teaching with authority (vv.21-22)

#### v.21
- **"They went into Capernaum":** "they" includes the four just called (compare 1:29). Their first lesson in following is watching what Jesus does.
- **A possible eyewitness trace:** C. H. Turner noticed Mark's pattern "they went... he entered" and suggested an eyewitness's "we went" retold. Richard Bauckham reads it as Peter's point of view. Suggestive, not conclusive.
- **Capernaum** (Greek: Καφαρναούμ, *Kapharnaoum*, G2584) means "village of Nahum." Its houses were rough black basalt grouped around shared courtyards. It was a village, not a city.
- **immediately** (Greek: εὐθύς, *euthys*, G2117) is Mark's signature word, about 41 times in the Gospel and 3 times in this passage (vv.21, 23, 28). Here it works less as a stopwatch and more as a signal that the next significant event is coming. The NIV has "when the Sabbath came."
- **synagogue** (Greek: συναγωγή, *synagōgē*, G4864) means "a gathering." The Sabbath assembly was mainly for reading and explaining Scripture. A pre-AD 70 inscription in Jerusalem says Theodotus "built the synagogue for the reading of the Law and the teaching of the commandments."
- **How a visitor could teach:** the synagogue ruler invited qualified men, sometimes visitors, to speak (Luke 4:16-21; Acts 13:14-15). Luke says Sabbath attendance was Jesus' custom (Luke 4:16).
- **"on the Sabbath day":** no one objects to Sabbath work here, unlike 3:1-6. The crowds wait until sundown to bring the sick (1:32).
- **Which building?** The white limestone synagogue visitors see today was built in the late fourth or fifth century. The first-century synagogue was "probably on or near this spot," but it has not been securely identified.

#### v.22
- **astonished** (Greek: ἐξεπλήσσοντο, *exeplēssonto*, from ἐκπλήσσω, *ekplēssō*, G1605) means "struck out" of one's composure, "dumbstruck." The imperfect tense pictures astonishment that keeps going.
- **authority** (Greek: ἐξουσία, *exousia*, G1849) is rightful authority, the right and power to act, not raw strength. In Daniel 7:14 (Greek) the one like a son of man receives an everlasting *exousia*.
- **the scribes** (Greek: γραμματεῖς, *grammateis*, G1122) were literate experts who copied, interpreted and applied the Law. Their authority was derived: it rested on the text and on interpretation handed down.
- **Careful with history:** the rabbinic habit of quoting a saying "in the name of" an earlier teacher is attested only from about AD 200, so don't read it back mechanically. The basic contrast still holds.
- **Mark records the effect, not the content.** He doesn't say what Jesus taught. Jesus had no formal training (6:2-3; John 7:15).
- **More than a prophet's manner:** even the prophets spoke as messengers ("Yahweh says"). The congregation may sense something more, though it can't yet name it.
- **Don't caricature the scribes.** The contrast doesn't condemn devout study (Sirach 39:1). One scribe is told, "You are not far from God's Kingdom" (12:34).
- **A thread to Jerusalem:** "astonished at his teaching" returns at 11:18, where the chief priests and scribes seek "how they might destroy him."

### 2. The spirit's outcry (vv.23-24)

#### v.23
- **"Immediately":** the interruption. The evil was there before Jesus arrived; his presence brought it into the open. Jesus doesn't go looking for it.
- **"in their synagogue":** an unclean presence inside the holy assembly, on holy time. Mark doesn't say whether anyone knew of the man's condition.
- **unclean spirit** (Greek: πνεῦμα ἀκάθαρτον, *pneuma akatharton*, G4151, G169) joins the language of evil spirits to Israel's purity laws: whatever is unfit for God's presence. The phrase echoes the Greek of Zechariah 13:2, where God promises to remove "the spirit of impurity" from the land "in that day."
- **"in" an unclean spirit:** the Greek says literally "a man in an unclean spirit," in its grip. John had promised that Jesus would baptize "in the Holy Spirit" (1:8). Two spirits meet in the synagogue.
- **The man** has no name and no words of his own. The voice that cries out is the spirit's. Once freed, he drops out of the story, and attention stays on Jesus.
- **Possession is not illness.** Mark distinguishes the sick from the demonized (1:32-34). The source calls this a spiritual affliction, not a misdiagnosed illness. Ancient people did not all blame spirits for every seizure; a Greek medical writer around 400 BC argued that epilepsy had natural causes.
- **His place in the community:** his condition was likely a burden and a source of shame to his household. Freedom would restore him to family and community life.

#### v.24
- **"Ha!"** (Greek: ἔα, *ea*) is a cry of dismay. It appears in the later manuscripts the WEB follows, not in the earliest manuscripts of Mark, though it is secure in Luke 4:34. No variant here changes the teaching.
- **"What do we have to do with you"** is a Hebrew idiom of protest: "Why are you interfering with us?" The widow says it to Elijah: "What have I to do with you, you man of God? You have come to me... to kill my son!" (1 Kings 17:18).
- **"we"... "I":** the spirit most likely speaks first for unclean spirits as a class, then for itself. Some read the plural as including the man.
- **destroy** (Greek: ἀπόλλυμι, *apollymi*, G622): many Jews expected God's coming reign to end the spirits' power (Testament of Moses 10:1). The spirit recognizes the judgment it has long expected, arriving in a person. The words can also be read as a statement: "You have come to destroy us!"
- **Irony:** later Jesus' human opponents plot "how they might destroy him" (3:6; 11:18).
- **"Jesus, you Nazarene"** is the ordinary name plus hometown. Nazareth was an obscure hamlet. The same title returns at the empty tomb: "Jesus, the Nazarene, who has been crucified" (16:6).
- **"the Holy One of God"** is rare (elsewhere only Luke 4:34; John 6:69). It means one wholly set apart to God, the exact opposite of "unclean." Some hear a Nazarene/Nazirite wordplay (Judges 13:7, Greek), but it can't be proven; hold it loosely. Don't press the title alone as a claim of deity. That claim rests on what Jesus does.
- **"I know you who you are":** many interpreters read the naming as a defensive move, since knowing a name was thought to give power over it. Plausible but debated. Either way, the knowledge is accurate and hostile: "The demons also believe, and shudder" (James 2:19).

### 3. The word that expels (vv.25-26)

#### v.25
- **rebuked** (Greek: ἐπιτιμάω, *epitimaō*, G2008) is how the Greek Old Testament renders God's own **rebuke** (Hebrew: גָּעַר, *ga'ar*, H1605) of hostile powers. God "rebuked the Red Sea also, and it was dried up" (Psalm 106:9).
- **No appeal to a higher name:** in Zechariah's vision the angel says, "Yahweh rebuke you, Satan!" (Zechariah 3:2). Michael says, "May the Lord rebuke you!" (Jude 9). Jesus simply rebukes. He exercises in his own person the prerogative angels refer back to God.
- **Be quiet** (Greek: φιμόω, *phimoō*, G5392) literally means "be muzzled," as in "You shall not muzzle the ox" (Deuteronomy 25:4). Mark uses it once more, when Jesus tells the sea, "Peace! Be still!" (4:39).
- **Five Greek words, seven in English.** No object, prayer, touch, incantation or higher name. Compare Eleazar's ring and root (Josephus, *Antiquities* 8.46-49) or the smoke of a fish's heart and liver (Tobit 8:2-3).
- **Why silence?** This is the first of Jesus' commands to silence (1:34; 3:11-12). He won't accept testimony from an evil source, and his identity is shown on his own terms, finally at the cross (15:39).
- **The spirit, not the man.** Jesus rebukes the spirit, not the man. Mark passes no moral judgment on the sufferer.
- **Shown, not stated:** the passage doesn't state that Jesus is divine. It shows him doing what Scripture reserves for God.

#### v.26
- **convulsing** (Greek: σπαράσσω, *sparassō*, G4682) means to tear or pull to and fro. In the Greek the spirit is the actor; the man is only the object of its violence.
- **"crying with a loud voice":** the spirit's words (v.24) become a wordless cry.
- **"came out of him"** answers Jesus' command (v.25): command, then compliance. The verb changes from command to report; the spirit does what he says.
- **The only sign.** Eleazar's demon was said to overturn a basin of water as proof. Here the convulsion is the only sign.
- **Not a manual.** The convulsion and cry show the spirit really left. They aren't a guide to how spirits behave. Mark's interest is who Jesus is.
- **The man remains.** The bondage was real, and it was not the man's true self. Mark doesn't describe him afterward; compare the man later seen "sitting, clothed, and in his right mind" (5:15).
- **A possible echo:** "loud voice" occurs in Mark only here, of Legion (5:7), and of Jesus on the cross (15:34, 37). Whether Mark wants us to hear them together is debated.

### 4. Verdict and report (vv.27-28)

#### v.27
- **amazed** (Greek: ἐθαμβήθησαν, *ethambēthēsan*, G2284) belongs to a word group found in the New Testament only in Mark. "All" is the strengthened form: absolutely everyone.
- **Amazement is not yet faith.** Mark never says they repented or believed, the response Jesus called for (1:15). At Nazareth the same astonishment turned to offense (6:2-3).
- **"What is this?"** The spirit knew who Jesus is; the crowd sees only what he has done. Later the disciples ask, "Who then is this, that even the wind and the sea obey him?" (4:41).
- **new** (Greek: καινός, *kainos*, G2537) tends to mean new in kind, not merely recent. God had promised, "Behold, I will do a new thing" (Isaiah 43:19).
- **A command called "teaching":** teaching and deed are one act of authority. "For he spoke, and it was done. He commanded, and it stood firm" (Psalm 33:9).
- **A text note:** the oldest manuscripts read, "A new teaching with authority!" The WEB follows later manuscripts that add "For." Either way, the teaching and the command express one authority.
- **command / obey** (Greek: ἐπιτάσσω, *epitassō*, G2004; ὑπακούω, *hypakouō*, G5219): Mark uses "obey" only here and of wind and sea (4:41). The spirits obey by compulsion. Disciples are called to "obedience of faith" (Romans 1:5), doing freely what the demons do unwillingly.

#### v.28
- **report** (Greek: ἀκοή, *akoē*, G189) literally means "hearing," what is heard. The same word appears in Isaiah 53:1 (Greek): "Who has believed our report?"
- **"went out" again:** the spirit went out of the man (v.26); now the news goes out into Galilee.
- **"surrounding area"** (Greek: περίχωρος, *perichōros*, G4066) may mean Galilee itself or the lands around it. Mark's next scenes stay in Galilee (1:39), which slightly favours the first reading.
- **Why the news spread fast:** Josephus counts 204 towns and villages in Galilee, linked by roads, markets and the lake. What happened in "their synagogue" was public by definition.
- **Fame is not faith.** Mark later shows that mere fame can hinder Jesus' mission (1:45). Good witness points to who Jesus is, not only to what is sensational.
- **The pattern set:** Jesus goes "preaching and casting out demons" through Galilee (1:39), and later gives the Twelve "authority over the unclean spirits" (6:7).

## Discussion

1. In vv.21-26, what does Jesus say and do, and what does he not use?
   - **Leader's note:** He enters, teaches, rebukes, silences and expels. His only words are "Be quiet, and come out of him!" (v.25). He uses no object, ring, root, incantation, prayer, touch or higher name, unlike Eleazar (Josephus) or Tobit's fish heart and liver. The crowd notices: "with authority he commands" (v.27).
2. The spirit knows exactly who Jesus is (v.24), while the crowd asks, "What is this?" (v.27). Why is knowing about Jesus not the same as trusting him?
   - **Leader's note:** The spirit's facts are right, but it wants Jesus at a distance ("What do we have to do with you?"). "The demons also believe, and shudder" (James 2:19). Classic Protestant theology names three parts of faith: knowledge, agreement and personal trust. The spirit lacks trust. The crowd sees the deed but not the person, and amazement is not yet faith (6:2-3). Mark presses toward "Who then is this?" (4:41) and the confession at the cross (15:39).
3. The crowd calls a command to an unclean spirit "a new teaching" (v.27). What does that tell us about Jesus' words?
   - **Leader's note:** In Jesus, word and deed are one act of authority. His word does what it says, as God's does (Psalm 33:9). He uses the same word to muzzle the sea (4:39-41). He doesn't borrow authority as the scribes did, or call on another name as the angel did (Zechariah 3:2). Later he says, "All authority has been given to me in heaven and on earth" (Matthew 28:18).
4. Jesus' word carries his own authority, and even unclean spirits obey it. When you hear Scripture read this week, how could you listen to it as his word to you, not just as good advice?
   - **Leader's note:** The source's application is a move from consulting Scripture to submitting to it, expecting it to change us and not only inform us (1 Thessalonians 2:13). Disciples obey freely and gladly (Romans 1:5), not by force like the spirits. Close with the passage's assurance: Jesus is stronger than whatever holds us or our neighbors. Be careful: do not link anyone's illness, disability or memory loss to evil spirits, or to weak faith. Mark keeps sickness and possession distinct (1:32-34) and passes no moral judgment on the man.

## Gaps in the Source

- The source does not discuss how faith relates to sickness or disability. These notes make no claim about it beyond the source's statements that Mark distinguishes sickness from demonization (1:32-34) and passes no moral judgment on the man.
- The source's Application section is written for congregations and church leaders (preaching, greeters, recovery groups). It has nothing specific to older adults or care settings; Discussion question 4 adapts its general principle (hearing Scripture as Christ's command) without adding new claims.
