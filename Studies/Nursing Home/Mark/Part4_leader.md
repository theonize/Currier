# Leader's Notes — Mark 1:21-28 - Authority over demons

## Verses

> **21** They went into Capernaum, and immediately on the Sabbath day he entered into the synagogue and taught.
>
> **22** They were astonished at his teaching, for he taught them as having authority, and not as the scribes.
>
> **23** Immediately there was in their synagogue a man with an unclean spirit, and he cried out,
>
> **24** saying, “Ha! What do we have to do with you, Jesus, you Nazarene? Have you come to destroy us? I know you who you are: the Holy One of God!”
>
> **25** Jesus rebuked him, saying, “Be quiet, and come out of him!”
>
> **26** The unclean spirit, convulsing him and crying with a loud voice, came out of him.
>
> **27** They were all amazed, so that they questioned among themselves, saying, “What is this? A new teaching? For with authority he commands even the unclean spirits, and they obey him!”
>
> **28** The report of him went out immediately everywhere into all the region of Galilee and its surrounding area.
>
> — *World English Bible (WEB)*

---

## Overview

- **Setting**
  - **When and where:** about AD 27-29, on a Sabbath, in the synagogue of Capernaum. This was a Jewish fishing and farming village on the northwest shore of the Sea of Galilee. The excavators estimated no more than about 1,500 people.
  - **Rulers:** Herod Antipas ruled Galilee (4 BC-AD 39) and had just imprisoned John (1:14; 6:17-29).
  - **First readers:** Most scholars date Mark about AD 65-70. Early tradition places it in Rome, after Nero's persecution. Some scholars propose Syria or Galilee. The passage's force doesn't depend on settling the question.
- **Author**
  - The Gospel is unnamed. Early tradition (Papias, quoted by Eusebius) gives it to John Mark, Peter's interpreter.
  - Capernaum was Peter's home (1:29). C. H. Turner and Richard Bauckham hear an eyewitness's "we went" behind "They went... he entered" (v.21). The argument is suggestive, not conclusive.
- **Context in Mark**
  - Jesus has announced that God's Kingdom is at hand (1:15) and called four fishermen (1:16-20). Their first experience of following him is watching his authority at work.
  - This scene opens the Capernaum day (1:21-39), which ends with Jesus "preaching and casting out demons" (1:39). That summary makes this scene the pattern of his ministry.
  - It plants threads Mark follows to Jerusalem: the scribes as foil, the question of who Jesus is, and the verb "destroy" (3:6; 11:18).
- **Genre and frame**
  - An exorcism story (vv.23-26) set inside a teaching scene (vv.21-22, 27). The frame interprets the center: the deed is the teaching made visible.
  - Mark keeps the usual form of an exorcism story and leaves out its usual tools: no object, no formula, no higher name.
  - The passage folds around v.25. The verbal links are firm. Whether Mark consciously built a formal chiasm is less certain.
- **Big idea:** In Jesus' first public act, the authority of his teaching proves itself in a bare command that expels an unclean spirit: God's holy and sovereign authority has arrived in person, and the Kingdom Jesus announced has begun its victory over evil.
- **Supporting movements**
  - On the Sabbath Jesus teaches in the Capernaum synagogue, astonishing hearers used to the derived authority of the scribes (vv.21-22).
  - An unclean spirit protests Jesus' intrusion, fears destruction, and names him "the Holy One of God" (vv.23-24).
  - Jesus muzzles the spirit with two commands, and it comes out, convulsing the man (vv.25-26).
  - The crowd calls this a new teaching that even unclean spirits obey, and the report spreads through Galilee (vv.27-28).
- **Guardrails for teaching this passage**
  - **Keep your eyes on Jesus.** C. S. Lewis warned against "an excessive and unhealthy interest" in devils (*The Screwtape Letters*, Preface). The passage spends almost all its attention on Jesus.
  - **Don't confuse illness with the demonic.** This text gives no warrant for treating epilepsy, mental illness, or disability as demonic by default (1:32-34). Say this plainly.
  - **Don't copy the method.** Jesus' bare command is his alone (see v.25).

## Key Verse

> **25** Jesus rebuked him, saying, “Be quiet, and come out of him!”

- **Why this verse:** The Literary section sets it at the center of the concentric pattern and the climax of the narrative arc, and the Theological section builds Jesus' divine prerogative on its verb "rebuked" (Overview §3; Literary §2; Theological §4).

## Info

### 1. Teaching with authority (vv.21-22)

#### v.21
- **"They went":** the plural implies the four fishermen came along (compare 1:29). They are silent witnesses here. Later they receive this same authority (3:15; 6:7).
- **Capernaum** (Greek: Καφαρναούμ, *Kapharnaoum*, G2584) comes from the Hebrew for "village of Nahum." Its houses were rough black basalt, grouped around shared courtyards.
- **"immediately"** (Greek: εὐθύς, *euthys*, G2117) appears three times in this passage (vv.21, 23, 28). Here it signals the next event more than it marks the clock. The NIV has "when the Sabbath came."
- **Sabbath:** Deuteronomy grounds it in Israel's release from slavery (Deuteronomy 5:15). Some interpreters find that a fitting backdrop for a Sabbath deliverance. Mark doesn't draw the link.
- **synagogue** (Greek: συναγωγή, *synagōgē*, G4864) means a gathering. Philo and Josephus describe the Sabbath meeting mainly as a time to read and explain the Law. A qualified man, sometimes a visitor, could be invited to speak (Luke 4:16-21; Acts 13:14-15). That explains how a teacher with no office could teach here.
- **Which building?** The white limestone synagogue visitors see today is later, no earlier than the late fourth century. Scholars disagree whether the basalt remains beneath it are first-century synagogue walls. The honest conclusion is "probably on or near this spot."
- **"taught":** Mark doesn't report what Jesus said, only how it struck them. Jesus' first public teaching in Mark is told by its effect.

#### v.22
- **astonished** (Greek: ἐκπλήσσω, *ekplēssō*, G1605) literally means "struck out" of one's composure. "Dumbstruck" captures the image. The tense pictures astonishment that kept going.
- **"for"** is the narrator's one step forward in the scene. His comment is the thesis. The event proves it, and the crowd repeats it (v.27).
- **authority** (Greek: ἐξουσία, *exousia*, G1849) means rightful authority, the freedom to act that belongs to a legitimate office. It is not raw strength. The word appears ten times in Mark, and these two uses (vv.22, 27) come first. Daniel 7:14 uses it in the Greek versions for the dominion given to one like a son of man.
- **scribes** (Greek: γραμματεύς, *grammateus*, G1122) were literate experts who copied, interpreted and applied the Law. Their authority was real but derived, resting on the text and on accepted interpretation.
- **A caution:** the habit of citing a saying "in the name of" an earlier sage is attested only from about AD 200. Don't read it back into AD 28 in detail. Mark himself says only that Jesus did not teach as they did.
- **Beyond the prophets:** the prophets introduced God's word with "Yahweh says" (Amos 1:3). Jesus uses no such preface, though he had no formal training (6:2-3; John 7:15).
- **Not an attack on study:** Ben Sira's ideal scribe "seeks out the wisdom of all the ancients" (Sirach 39:1). The contrast doesn't condemn that labor. One scribe is later told, "You are not far from God's Kingdom" (12:34).
- **Looking ahead:** astonishment returns at Nazareth, where it turns to offense (6:2-3), and in Jerusalem: "all the multitude was astonished at his teaching" (11:18).

### 2. The spirit's outcry (vv.23-24)

#### v.23
- **"Immediately"** marks the intrusion. Jesus does not go looking for the spirit. His presence forces the confrontation.
- **"their synagogue":** this was the village's own institution, so what happened there was public. The phrase may also quietly distance the narrator from it (compare 1:39).
- **unclean spirit** (Greek: πνεῦμα ἀκάθαρτον, *pneuma akatharton*, G4151, G169) joins spirit language to Israel's purity system. "Unclean" meant unfit for God's presence (Leviticus 10:10).
- **An end-time echo:** only one Old Testament text joins the two words. In "that day" God will cause "the spirit of impurity to pass out of the land" (Zechariah 13:2). Its Greek is exactly Mark's term. Hearers who knew Scripture might have sensed that day arriving.
- **"with an unclean spirit"** is literally "in an unclean spirit" (Greek: ἐν πνεύματι ἀκαθάρτῳ, *en pneumati akathartō*), meaning in its grip. It mirrors John's promise of baptism "in the Holy Spirit" (1:8). Two spirits, two spheres of control.
- **The man:** he has no name and no words of his own. His condition likely threatened his place in family and community. Mark doesn't say whether anyone knew of it.
- **Not a diagnosis:** Mark distinguishes the sick from the demonized (1:32-34). The passage presents a spiritual affliction, not a misdiagnosed illness, and gives no warrant for calling illness demonic by default.

#### v.24
- **"Ha!"** (Greek: ἔα, *ea*, G1436) is a cry of dismay. It stands in the Byzantine manuscripts the WEB follows here, not in the earliest manuscripts of Mark. It is secure in Luke 4:34. The teaching doesn't change.
- **"What do we have to do with you"** (Greek: Τί ἡμῖν καὶ σοί, *ti hēmin kai soi*) is a Semitic idiom of protest: "Why are you interfering with us?" Compare the widow to Elijah: "You have come to me... to kill my son!" (1 Kings 17:18).
- **"us," then "I":** the spirit most likely speaks first for unclean spirits as a class, then for itself. Some think it includes the man.
- **"destroy"** (Greek: ἀπόλλυμι, *apollymi*, G622) means destroy utterly. Many Jews expected God's reign to end the spirits' power (Testament of Moses 10:1, not Scripture). Greek manuscripts have no question marks, so it may also be read as an accusation: "You have come to destroy us!"
- **A word that returns:** the Pharisees plot "how they might destroy him" (3:6), and so do the chief priests and scribes (11:18). This irony is drawn from Mark's repeated vocabulary, not from any comment of his.
- **"you Nazarene"** (Greek: Ναζαρηνός, *Nazarēnos*, G3479) is the ordinary formula of name plus hometown. The same title returns at the tomb: "Jesus, the Nazarene, who has been crucified" (16:6).
- **"the Holy One of God"** (Greek: ὁ ἅγιος τοῦ θεοῦ, *ho hagios tou theou*) is rare (elsewhere John 6:69; Luke 4:34). It means one wholly set apart to God, the opposite of "unclean." The phrase alone shouldn't be pressed to claim deity. That claim rests on what Jesus does in the scene. A possible play on "Nazirite" (Judges 13:7) should be held lightly.
- **"I know you who you are":** many interpreters, following Otto Bauernfeind, read this as a defensive move, since naming an opponent was thought to give power. Others see only terrified recognition. The text doesn't require the first reading. Either way, it fails.
- **True words, no trust:** the spirit is more accurate than the crowd, yet its confession is a cry of fear. "The demons also believe, and shudder" (James 2:19).

### 3. The word that expels (vv.25-26)

#### v.25
- **rebuked** (Greek: ἐπιτιμάω, *epitimaō*, G2008) is how the Greek Old Testament renders God's own **rebuke** (Hebrew: גָּעַר, *ga'ar*, H1605) of hostile powers. He "rebuked the Red Sea also, and it was dried up" (Psalm 106:9). Howard Clark Kee argued it had become a word for mastering evil spirits. Here it means mastery, not moral correction.
- **Others defer:** the angel says, "Yahweh rebuke you, Satan!" (Zechariah 3:2). Michael says, "May the Lord rebuke you!" (Jude 9). Jesus simply rebukes. The passage does not state that he is divine. It shows him doing what Scripture reserves for God.
- **Be quiet** (Greek: φιμόω, *phimoō*, G5392) literally means "be muzzled," as in "You shall not muzzle the ox" (Deuteronomy 25:4). Mark uses it once more, to the sea (4:39).
- **Few words:** five in Greek, seven in the WEB. These are the only words Jesus speaks in the passage, and the spirit uses far more.
- **What is missing:** Tobit tells of smoke from a fish's heart and liver (Tobit 8:2-3). Josephus tells of Eleazar with a ring and a root (*Antiquities* 8.46-49). Jesus uses no object, no formula, and no higher name.
- **Why silence him?** This is the first of Jesus' commands to silence (later 1:34, 44; 3:12; 8:30). He won't accept testimony from an unclean source, and his identity can't be rightly understood apart from the cross. Compare the centurion at 15:39.
- **His alone:** a bare command in his own name belongs to Jesus. His followers act by his commission and through prayer (3:14-15; 6:7; 9:28-29), never using his name as a spell (Acts 19:13-16). Christians differ on how directly the church should engage such things today.
- **Spirit, not man:** Jesus rebukes the spirit. Mark passes no moral judgment on the sufferer. The captive is not the enemy.

#### v.26
- **convulsing** (Greek: σπαράσσω, *sparassō*, G4682) means to tear or pull to and fro. Mark uses it here and in his last exorcism (9:26).
- **The spirit is the actor:** the Greek participles agree with "spirit." The man is only the object of its violence. The power that held him shows itself only as it is broken.
- **"came out of him"** repeats the command with the same verb, preposition and pronoun (v.25). The word is obeyed exactly.
- **Not a manual:** the convulsion and cry show the spirit really left. Mark's interest is in who Jesus is, not in how spirits behave.
- **"loud voice"** (Greek: φωνῇ μεγάλῃ, *phōnē megalē*) occurs in Mark only at 1:26, 5:7, 15:34 and 15:37: twice of spirits, twice of Jesus on the cross. Whether Mark wants us to hear them together is debated.
- **The man afterward:** Mark doesn't describe the man afterward. He drops out the moment he is free, and attention stays on the one who freed him. The Gerasene shows what such freedom restores: "sitting, clothed, and in his right mind" (5:15).
- **An analogy, with care:** breaking with long-held bondage often gets worse before it gets better. That is a pastoral analogy, not a claim that every struggle is demonic.

### 4. Verdict and report (vv.27-28)

#### v.27
- **amazed** (Greek: θαμβέομαι, *thambeomai*, G2284) is found in the New Testament only in Mark. "All" is strengthened: absolutely everyone.
- **Wonder is not yet faith:** Mark never says they repented or believed, the response Jesus called for (1:15). Amazement is where wisdom can begin.
- **questioned among themselves** (Greek: συζητέω, *syzēteō*, G4802): they ask the right first question, "What is this?" They see authority, not yet identity.
- **"A new teaching?"** Mark's first exhibit of Jesus' teaching is a command a spirit obeys. The crowd treats a deed as a teaching.
- **new** (Greek: καινός, *kainos*, G2537) tends to mean new in kind, not merely the latest. That is a tendency, not a rule. God had promised newness: "Behold, I will do a new thing" (Isaiah 43:19).
- **A text note:** many translations read "A new teaching with authority!" The WEB attaches "with authority" to "he commands." Either way, teaching and command express one authority. No variant here changes the teaching.
- **"even the unclean spirits":** the word "even" escalates. If spirits obey him, how much more should people listen.
- **obey** (Greek: ὑπακούω, *hypakouō*, G5219) means "listen under." Mark uses it only here and at 4:41: "Who then is this, that even the wind and the sea obey him?" The question moves from "What?" to "Who?"
- **Forced or free:** the spirits obey by compulsion. Disciples are called to "obedience of faith" (Romans 1:5), doing freely what the demons do unwillingly.

#### v.28
- **report** (Greek: ἀκοή, *akoē*, G189) literally means "hearing," so "what is heard." Isaiah 53:1 uses the same word in the Greek: "Who has believed our report?" Within Mark it is still reputation, not faith.
- **"went out"** (Greek: ἐξέρχομαι, *exerchomai*, G1831) is the verb of vv.25-26. The spirit goes out of the man, and the news goes out into Galilee.
- **"immediately everywhere... all the region":** stacked phrases enlarge the impact. One man in one building becomes a whole region.
- **Why so fast:** the synagogue was the town's meeting place, and Josephus counts 204 towns and villages in Galilee (*Life* 235). In that dense web a report could travel quickly.
- **surrounding area** (Greek: περίχωρος, *perichōros*, G4066) may mean Galilee itself or the lands around it. Mark's next scenes stay within Galilee (1:39), which slightly favors the first reading.
- **Fame has limits:** mere fame can hinder Jesus' mission (1:45). The aim is witness that points to who Jesus is, not only to what is sensational.
- **Next:** "when they had come out of the synagogue" (1:29), the same verb carries them to Simon's house.

## Discussion

1. In vv.21-28, what does Jesus do, and how do the spirit and the people respond?
   - **Leader's note:** Jesus enters, teaches, rebukes, silences and expels. The spirit protests, names him, and comes out. The people are astonished (v.22), then amazed and questioning (v.27), and the report spreads (v.28). Point out what Jesus doesn't do: no object, no formula, no higher name. Keep the group's eyes on him, not on the demon.
2. The spirit says true words about Jesus, yet Jesus tells it, "Be quiet" (vv.24-25). Why is knowing the right words about Jesus not enough?
   - **Leader's note:** The spirit knows his name, his hometown and his holiness, but its confession is a cry of fear, not of trust (James 2:19). Faith is more than information. It is personal trust in him. Paul likewise silenced a spirit that said true things (Acts 16:16-18). Ask gently: can we say true things about Jesus and still keep him at arm's length?
3. The crowd was amazed, but Mark never says they believed (vv.27-28). What is the difference between being amazed at Jesus and following him?
   - **Leader's note:** The same astonishment turned to offense at Nazareth (6:2-3). The four fishermen of the last lesson did something different: they left their nets (1:16-20). Hearing should ripen into trust and obedience: "This is my beloved Son. Listen to him" (9:7). Help the group move from "What is this?" to "Who is this?" (4:41).
4. Jesus is stronger than whatever holds us. Where you are now, what is one fear or burden you could bring to him in prayer?
   - **Leader's note:** Offer to pray with anyone who wishes. Draw near to God (James 4:7-8), pray Scripture, recall Christ's finished victory (Colossians 2:15), and ask others to pray with you. Don't suggest that anyone's illness, of body or mind, comes from a demon. If someone reports experiences they believe are spiritual oppression, listen carefully, don't rush to a diagnosis, and involve their pastor and medical and mental-health professionals alongside prayer. The afflicted deserve compassion, not spectacle.
