# Leader's Notes — Mark 1:9-13 - Baptism and temptation

## Verses

> **9** In those days, Jesus came from Nazareth of Galilee, and was baptized by John in the Jordan.
>
> **10** Immediately coming up from the water, he saw the heavens parting, and the Spirit descending on him like a dove.
>
> **11** A voice came out of the sky, “You are my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased.”
>
> **12** Immediately the Spirit drove him out into the wilderness.
>
> **13** He was there in the wilderness forty days tempted by Satan. He was with the wild animals; and the angels were serving him.
>
> — *World English Bible (WEB)*

---

## Overview

- **Setting**
  - **When and where:** about AD 27-29, at the lower Jordan, traditionally opposite Jericho. Luke dates John's call to "the fifteenth year of the reign of Tiberius" (Luke 3:1-2). The exact year is debated.
  - **Rulers:** Herod Antipas ruled Galilee; the prefect Pontius Pilate governed Judea.
  - **First readers:** Mark was most likely written about AD 64-70 for mostly Gentile believers in Rome. Some scholars propose Syria.
- **Author**
  - The Gospel is unnamed. Early tradition (Papias, quoted by Eusebius) gives it to John Mark, Peter's interpreter.
  - Peter wasn't present at these events, but the order matches his preaching: John's baptism, the Spirit's anointing, then Galilee (Acts 10:37-38).
  - No human witnessed the testing. If the account is historical tradition, it goes back to Jesus' own report.
- **Context in Mark**
  - It follows John's promise of one "mightier" who will baptize with the Holy Spirit (Mark 1:7-8).
  - Almost every key word has already sounded in 1:1-8: come, baptize, Spirit, wilderness, messenger/angel, straight/immediately, Jordan. The scene fulfills John's announcement.
  - Next come John's arrest and Jesus' preaching in Galilee (1:14-15). Scholars debate whether the prologue ends at v.13 or v.15.
- **Genre and frame**
  - It is gospel narrative inside Mark's prologue. Verses 10-11 are a heavenly vision and installation scene; vv.12-13 are a compressed testing account with apocalyptic coloring.
  - The prologue is a key to the book. The reader hears heaven's verdict before any human character acknowledges it. That dramatic irony runs to the centurion's confession at the cross (15:39).
  - The style is spare: 83 Greek words, every verse opening with "and," and Mark's first two uses of "immediately."
- **Big idea:** At the Jordan God tears open the heavens to name Jesus his beloved, Spirit-anointed Son, and the same Spirit at once drives him into the wilderness to be tested by Satan, so that the Son's identity is declared before any work and proved in conflict.
- **Supporting movements**
  - Jesus comes from obscure Nazareth and is baptized among people confessing their sins (v.9).
  - The heavens are torn, the Spirit descends, and the Father names him, all before any public ministry (vv.10-11).
  - The Spirit drives him into the wilderness, where Satan tests him for forty days among wild animals while angels serve him. No outcome is narrated (vv.12-13).
- **A frame around the whole Gospel:** the heavens torn here (1:10) pair with the temple veil torn at Jesus' death (15:38). These are Mark's only two uses of "tear."

## Info

### 1. The Son with sinners (v.9)

#### v.9
- **"In those days"** is a biblical story formula. It ties Jesus to John's ministry and echoes the grown Moses going out to his brothers (Exodus 2:11).
- **"Jesus came"** answers John's "after me comes he who is mightier" (1:7). The promised one arrives in the next sentence.
- **Nazareth** was a village of a few hundred people, absent from the Old Testament and Josephus (compare John 1:46).
- **Only from Galilee:** the crowds came from Judea and Jerusalem (1:5). Jesus is the one Mark names as coming from Galilee.
- **The journey** was several days on foot, down about 750 m (2,500 ft). He left his trade and his household's income behind (inference).
- **baptized** (Greek: βαπτίζω, *baptizō*, G907) means "immerse, dip." It is the same verb used of Naaman in the Jordan (2 Kings 5:14, Greek Old Testament).
- **A rite of repentance:** John's baptism was performed by another person, apparently once, and tied to repentance (1:4). Mark gives no reason why Jesus received it. It is identification, not confession (2 Corinthians 5:21; Hebrews 4:15).
- **Israel's threshold river:** Israel crossed the Jordan under Joshua, whose Greek name is Ἰησοῦς (Joshua 3-4). Elisha and Naaman also met God there (2 Kings 2; 5). Mark doesn't develop these links.

### 2. Heaven torn, Son named (vv.10-11)

#### v.10
- **"Immediately"** (Greek: εὐθύς, *euthys*, G2117) is used here for the first time of about forty in Mark, and it marks a hinge. It may echo "make his paths *straight*" (1:3), but that can't be pressed.
- **Up and down:** Jesus comes *up* as the Spirit comes *down*.
- **torn** (Greek: σχίζω, *schizō*, G4977) is the verb for ripping cloth. The WEB's "parting" softens it. Jesus sees the heavens *being torn*.
- **Isaiah's prayer answered:** "Oh that you would tear the heavens, that you would come down" (Isaiah 64:1). The Hebrew verb is **tear** (Hebrew: קָרַע, *qaraʿ*, H7167).
- **Two tearings:** Mark uses "tear" only here and at the temple veil (15:38). Each is followed by a declaration that Jesus is God's Son (1:11; 15:39).
- **"the Spirit"** points back to 1:8. The coming Spirit-baptizer first receives the Spirit (Isaiah 42:1; Acts 10:38).
- **like a dove** (Greek: περιστερά, *peristera*, G4058) may describe the manner of descent or the form seen. The dove was the humble offering of the poor (Leviticus 12:8).
- **The silence ends:** some Jews felt that prophecy had ceased (1 Maccabees 9:27). Torn heavens and a direct voice signal that the long silence was over.

#### v.11
- **The only speech in the passage.** Jesus says nothing until 1:15.
- **"You are"** is spoken *to* Jesus. Matthew has "This is" (Matthew 3:17). In Mark the reader overhears.
- **A chord of Scripture:** the royal son (Psalm 2:7), the Servant in whom God delights (Isaiah 42:1), and possibly Isaac (Genesis 22:2), which is less certain.
- **beloved** (Greek: ἀγαπητός, *agapētos*, G27) renders "only" (Hebrew: יָחִיד, *yachid*, H3173) in Genesis 22 in the Greek Old Testament. Mark uses it at 1:11, 9:7 and 12:6, where the beloved son is killed.
- **well pleased** (Greek: εὐδοκέω, *eudokeō*, G2106) means delight and choice. The verdict comes before any recorded deed.
- **Declared, not conferred:** "You *are*," and Mark omits "Today I have become your father." Jesus does not become the Son at his baptism.
- **Father, Son and Spirit** appear in one scene. Mark has no Trinity doctrine, but the church rightly read God's triune self-revelation here.
- **Roman echo:** Tiberius' coins called him "son of the divine Augustus." This voice from heaven names another Son.

### 3. The Son driven and tested (vv.12-13)

#### v.12
- **"Immediately" again:** there is no gap between love declared and testing.
- **drove out** (Greek: ἐκβάλλω, *ekballō*, G1544) is Mark's verb for casting out demons (1:34, 39). Matthew and Luke soften it to "led."
- **The force of the verb** is urgency and divine compulsion, not hostility.
- **The Spirit links the two panels:** it descends (v.10), then drives (v.12).
- **wilderness** (Greek: ἔρημος, *erēmos*, G2048) was the place of testing and new beginning (Deuteronomy 8:2; Hosea 2:14), and also the haunt of evil powers.
- **Spirit-led does not mean led into comfort.**

#### v.13
- **Forty days** is the only "forty" in Mark. It recalls Israel (Deuteronomy 8:2), Moses (Exodus 34:28) and Elijah (1 Kings 19:8). Mark doesn't mention fasting.
- **tempted** (Greek: πειράζω, *peirazō*, G3985) means both "test" and "tempt," and the testing runs through all forty days. God tests to prove; Satan tempts to destroy; God tempts no one (James 1:13).
- **Satan** (Greek: Σατανᾶς, *Satanas*, G4567) means "adversary." He is Mark's first named enemy, and he is given no lines.
- **wild animals** (Greek: θηρίον, *thērion*, G2342) suggest danger (Psalm 91:11-13) or messianic peace (Isaiah 11:6-9). Either way, the Son is unharmed.
- **serving** (Greek: διακονέω, *diakoneō*, G1247) is ongoing care, like the angel feeding Elijah (1 Kings 19:5-8). Compare Mark 10:45.
- **No victory narrated:** the outcome shows in Jesus' first exorcism (1:21-28) and in "the strong man" bound (3:27).
- **A possible new-Adam echo:** Adam was "driven out" of Eden with the same verb in the Greek Old Testament (Genesis 3:24). This is suggestive, not decisive.
- **Nero's Rome:** Christians there were killed by beasts and dogs (Tacitus, *Annals* 15.44). "With the wild animals" may have spoken to them, though this can't be proven.

## Discussion

1. In these five verses, what does Jesus himself do, and what is done to him or said over him?
   - **Leader's note:** Jesus' only active verbs are "came," "coming up," "saw" and "was." He is baptized, addressed, driven, tested and served, and he speaks no word. Heaven names and sustains him before he does any public work.
2. Why might Mark place the Father's "You are my beloved Son" *immediately* before the Spirit drives Jesus into the wilderness?
   - **Leader's note:** "Immediately" joins the voice and the wilderness without a pause. The beloved are not exempt from the wilderness, and love and testing are not opposites (Proverbs 3:12). Identity is declared, then proved in conflict, and the thread runs on to the "beloved son" of 12:6.
3. Jesus had no sin, yet he stepped into a river where people were confessing their sins. What does that tell us about him?
   - **Leader's note:** This is the Son standing where sinners stand (2 Corinthians 5:21; Hebrews 4:15), the start of his eating with tax collectors (2:15-17). Mark gives no reason, so don't fill that silence with any suggestion that Jesus sinned.
4. Have you known a hard season that came right after a real blessing? Where did you see God's care for you there?
   - **Leader's note:** Testing after blessing is not proof that God has withdrawn (1 Peter 4:12); it is the path the beloved Son walked. A modern "wilderness" may be a hospital room or an empty house. God's care often comes through other people, and receiving help is not weakness. Jesus "is able to help those who are tempted" (Hebrews 2:18).
