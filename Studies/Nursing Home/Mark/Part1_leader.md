# Leader's Notes — Mark 1:1-8 - John the Baptist

## Verses

> **1** The beginning of the Good News of Jesus Christ, the Son of God.
>
> **2** As it is written in the prophets, “Behold, I send my messenger before your face, who will prepare your way before you:
>
> **3** the voice of one crying in the wilderness, ‘Make ready the way of the Lord! Make his paths straight!’”
>
> **4** John came baptizing in the wilderness and preaching the baptism of repentance for forgiveness of sins.
>
> **5** All the country of Judea and all those of Jerusalem went out to him. They were baptized by him in the Jordan river, confessing their sins.
>
> **6** John was clothed with camel’s hair and a leather belt around his waist. He ate locusts and wild honey.
>
> **7** He preached, saying, “After me comes he who is mightier than I, the thong of whose sandals I am not worthy to stoop down and loosen.
>
> **8** I baptized you in water, but he will baptize you in the Holy Spirit.”
>
> — *World English Bible (WEB)*

---

## Overview

- **Setting of the events:** John's preaching begins in "the fifteenth year of Tiberius" (Luke 3:1-3), c. AD 28-29 (AD 26-27 on a co-regency count). Place: the Judean wilderness and the lower Jordan near Jericho. Pilate governs Judea, Herod Antipas rules Galilee and Perea, and Caiaphas is high priest.
- **Setting of the writing:** Mark is often dated in the AD 60s or early 70s. Early tradition (Papias, Irenaeus, Clement) names John Mark, "the interpreter of Peter," writing in Rome for a largely Gentile church. Those readers were under pressure: after the fire of AD 64, Nero killed many Christians (Tacitus, *Annals* 15.44).
- **Author and immediate context:** Mark has no birth story and no genealogy. He begins where the apostles' preaching began, "from the baptism of John" (Acts 1:22; 10:37). Verse 1 is the book's title. Verses 2-8 are the first panel of the prologue (1:1-13 or 1:1-15), and the next scene answers this one at once: John says one "comes," and then "Jesus came" (1:9). John promises the Spirit, and the Spirit descends on Jesus (1:10).
- **Genre and frame:** This is Gospel narrative, an ancient "life" shaped by proclamation. It opens with a title line, a scripture citation ("As it is written"), a short report of a prophet's ministry, and John's one announcement. The prologue tells the reader what the characters do not yet know: who Jesus is.
- **Jesus offstage:** Jesus is named in v.1 and then stays out of view until 1:9. Even so, he is the subject throughout, as the "you" whose way is prepared (v.2), "the Lord" (v.3), and "he who is mightier" (v.7).
- **Big idea:** Mark opens the Good News of Jesus Christ with God keeping his written promise. The Elijah-like messenger appears in the wilderness, calls Israel to repent and confess, and points away from himself to a mightier one. On the widely held reading, that one's coming is the Lord's own coming, and he will baptize in the Holy Spirit.
- **Supporting movements:**
  - **The headline** (v.1): Good News whose content is Jesus, the Messiah and Son of God.
  - **The promise** (vv.2-3): God speaks first, sending a messenger to prepare the Lord's way.
  - **The messenger** (vv.4-6): John appears as that voice, the crowds come confessing, and his clothes mark him as Elijah.
  - **The pointer** (vv.7-8): John ranks himself below a slave and announces the one who will baptize in the Holy Spirit.

## Key Verse

> **8** I baptized you in water, but he will baptize you in the Holy Spirit.”

- **Why this verse:** The research treats it as the climax of the passage: a balanced contrast (I/he, water/Spirit, past/future) that every earlier pair has prepared for. The Theological Analysis builds on it to show the Coming One doing what the prophets said only God would do.

## Info

### 1. The headline (v.1)

#### v.1
- The verse has no verb. It is a title line, like the Septuagint's "Beginning of the word of the Lord to Hosea" (Hosea 1:2 LXX).
- **Beginning** (Greek: ἀρχή, *archē*, G746) can mean the opening section or the foundation of the good news. Readers of the Greek Bible may also hear "In the beginning" (Genesis 1:1 LXX). The source treats that echo as possible, not certain.
- **Good News** (Greek: εὐαγγέλιον, *euangelion*, G2098) is first of all Isaiah's word, for the herald who announces God's return: "Behold, your God!" (Isaiah 40:9; 52:7). Rome used the same word for imperial announcements; the Priene inscription (9 BC) hailed Augustus's birthday as the start of "good tidings." Whether Mark meant a deliberate counterclaim is debated, and the Isaiah background is firmer.
- **Christ** (Greek: Χριστός, *Christos*, G5547) translates Hebrew *mashiach*, "anointed one," the promised king from David's line (Psalm 2:2).
- "Son of God" was a royal title in Israel (Psalm 2:7). Augustus also called himself *divi filius*, "son of the deified one," on his coins.
- **Textual note:** A few early witnesses lack "the Son of God" (the first hand of Codex Sinaiticus; Origen). Vaticanus and most others include it, and the source judges the longer reading more probable. No teaching depends on it, because Mark calls Jesus the Son of God at 1:11, 9:7 and 15:39.
- **Frame:** The narrator's "Son of God" (1:1) is answered by the centurion's "Truly this man was the Son of God!" (15:39). The reader knows from the first line what the characters learn slowly.
- **Leader cue:** If anyone's Bible has a footnote here, reassure them that the variant does not change what Mark teaches about Jesus.

### 2. The promise (vv.2-3)

#### v.2
- **As it is written** (Greek: γέγραπται, *gegraptai*) is a perfect tense, meaning "it stands written." God's word was given long ago and is still in force. The story begins with God's promise, not with John or Jesus.
- One citation joins three texts: Exodus 23:20 and Malachi 3:1 (v.2), then Isaiah 40:3 (v.3). In Hebrew, Malachi and Isaiah share the same "clear the way" wording. Joining texts by shared words was ordinary Jewish practice.
- **Textual note:** The earliest manuscripts read "in Isaiah the prophet." The WEB follows later manuscripts that read "in the prophets." Naming the principal source was ancient practice, and it puts the whole passage under Isaiah 40. The difference affects no doctrine.
- **Messenger** (Greek: ἄγγελος, *angelos*, G32) can mean a human envoy or an angel. Malachi's own name means "my messenger."
- **The pronoun shift:** In Malachi the messenger prepares the way "before *me*," meaning the LORD's own coming. Mark reads "before *your* face ... *your* way," so God is now speaking to another person. The source cautiously sees here God speaking to his Son, which prepares for "You are my beloved Son" (1:11).
- God is the first speaker in Mark: "Behold, I send." He sends before anyone seeks him.

#### v.3
- Mark follows the Greek of Isaiah 40:3, which places the *voice* "in the wilderness." That fits John exactly. The Qumran community followed the Hebrew sense and withdrew to the desert to study the Law (1QS 8.12-16).
- **Royal-road image:** Before a king arrived, valleys were filled and routes cleared, as on Babylon's Processional Way or for a Roman *adventus*. "Prepare the way" means getting ready for a sovereign.
- **The Lord** (Greek: κύριος, *kyrios*, G2962) stands for the divine name Yahweh in Isaiah 40:3. Mark changes "paths of our God" to "*his* paths," so "the Lord" can be read as Jesus, named in v.1. The wording is fact. The identification is an inference, though commentators across traditions draw it (Marcus, Watts, France); others read Jesus as the agent through whom Yahweh comes.
- Isaiah 40 opens with "Comfort, comfort my people" and "her iniquity is pardoned" (Isaiah 40:1-2). At root this voice speaks comfort. Malachi adds that the Lord's coming also refines (Malachi 3:1-3).
- **Way** (Greek: ὁδός, *hodos*, G3598) plants a theme: Jesus later walks "on the way" to Jerusalem and the cross (8:27; 10:32, 52).
- The commands are plural. They are addressed to all the hearers, not only to John.
- **Leader cue:** Linger on Isaiah 40:1-2. For this group, the passage begins with comfort before it calls for repentance.

### 3. The messenger (vv.4-6)

#### v.4
- "In the wilderness" comes straight back from v.3. The narrative says, in effect, "this is the voice."
- **Repentance** (Greek: μετάνοια, *metanoia*, G3341) is more than regret. It is the prophets' call to "return" (Hebrew: שׁוּב, *shuv*, H7725): "Return to me, and I will return to you" (Malachi 3:7).
- **Forgiveness** (Greek: ἄφεσις, *aphesis*, G859) means "release." It is the Jubilee word (Leviticus 25:10) and the "release to the captives" of Isaiah 61:1.
- "For forgiveness" names the goal. The rite expresses a repentance that looks to God for forgiveness, and the text does not say the water itself conveys it. Jesus later pronounces forgiveness (2:5) and grounds it in his death (10:45; 14:24).
- Forgiveness was normally sought through the temple sacrifices, but here it is announced at a river about twenty miles away. Mark does not present this as an attack on the temple.
- Josephus confirms John's ministry independently: "a good man" who joined baptism to righteous living (*Antiquities* 18.116-119).

#### v.5
- "All ... all" is hyperbole for an overwhelming response, not a census.
- From Jerusalem it was about 20-22 miles and a drop of more than 3,600 feet down the bandit-prone Jericho road. Going out took a real decision. The imperfect verb pictures a steady stream of people.
- They "were baptized *by him*." Ordinary Jewish washing was done to oneself, but John performed this rite on others, which is likely why he was called "the Baptizer."
- **Confessing** (Greek: ἐξομολογέω, *exomologeō*, G1843) means acknowledging openly and aloud. The same verb is used for praise. In both cases it means telling the truth out loud.
- Repentance here is both personal ("their sins") and communal (a whole region goes out together).
- The Jordan carried memories of Joshua's crossing, Elijah being taken up, and Naaman washed clean. The scene *may* evoke Israel entering the land again, cleansed. That is an inference; Mark does not say it.
- Irony: Jerusalem's crowds go out now, but its leaders later refuse to say whether John's baptism was "from heaven, or from men" (11:30).

#### v.6
- "A leather belt around his waist" repeats almost word for word the Septuagint's description of Elijah (2 Kings 1:8). Mark shows that John is the Elijah figure rather than saying so.
- Malachi promised Elijah before the day of the LORD (Malachi 4:5-6). Jesus implies the identification in Mark 9:11-13, and Matthew 17:13 states it.
- John is not Elijah returned in person (John 1:21). He comes "in the spirit and power of Elijah" (Luke 1:17).
- Locusts were clean food under the Law (Leviticus 11:22). "Wild honey" may be from wild bees or a sweet tree sap. Either way, John ate food gathered, not grown or bought.
- **Guardrail:** The point of v.6 is who John is, not a diet or dress code. Jesus himself was contrasted with John because he "came eating and drinking" (Matthew 11:18-19). The source warns against allegorizing the locusts and honey.

### 4. The pointer (vv.7-8)

#### v.7
- **Mightier** (Greek: ἰσχυρότερος, *ischyroteros*, G2478) echoes Isaiah 40:10 LXX, "the Lord comes with strength." The verb "comes" and the root "strong" both match. Later Jesus is the one who binds "the strong man" (3:27).
- Untying a master's sandals was a slave's job. A later rabbinic saying excused even disciples from it (Babylonian Talmud *Ketubot* 96a), so John ranks himself below a slave.
- **Worthy** (Greek: ἱκανός, *hikanos*, G2425) means "fit, adequate," as in Moses' "I am not adequate" (Exodus 4:10 LXX). John is speaking of his unfitness for the task, not expressing self-hatred.
- Only Mark includes "stoop down." John pictures himself bent to the ground and still unfit.
- **After me** (Greek: ὀπίσω μου, *opisō mou*) is also Mark's language for discipleship ("Come after me," 1:17; 8:34). The one who comes "after" John, in a follower's place, is the greater.
- John's humility is accurate self-assessment, not low self-esteem: "He must increase, but I must decrease" (John 3:30).

#### v.8
- The verse is a tight contrast: "I" against "he," "baptized" against "will baptize," water against Holy Spirit. Every earlier pair in the passage has prepared for it.
- In the prophets, pouring out the Spirit is God's own act (Isaiah 44:3; Ezekiel 36:27; Joel 2:28). The Coming One will do what God promised to do himself.
- To **baptize** (Greek: βαπτίζω, *baptizō*, G907) is to immerse. Because the prophets speak of the Spirit "poured out," being immersed in the Spirit is a natural picture of abundance.
- The contrast is between a preparatory sign and the reality it points to, not between a false baptism and a true one. John's baptism is not Christian baptism (Acts 19:1-7).
- Mark, unlike Matthew and Luke, does not add "and fire," so John's saying stresses the gift.
- The Spirit first descends on Jesus (1:10): the one who will give the Spirit first receives it. Mark never narrates the outpouring, so the promise reaches past the end of the book. It is fulfilled at Pentecost, when Jesus pours out the promised Spirit (Acts 1:5; 2:33).
- **Leader cue:** Traditions differ on how Spirit-baptism relates to conversion. The source says this verse does not settle that debate, but it does settle who the giver is.

## Discussion

1. Jesus never appears in these eight verses, yet Mark says the passage is about him. Where can you find Jesus "hidden" in vv.1-8?
   - **Leader's note:** He is named in v.1, then appears as the "you" whose way is prepared (v.2), "the Lord" whose paths are made straight (v.3), and "he who is mightier" who will baptize in the Holy Spirit (vv.7-8). Even when John holds the stage, the story points to Jesus.

2. The "voice in the wilderness" comes from Isaiah 40, which begins, "Comfort, comfort my people... her iniquity is pardoned" (Isaiah 40:1-2). How does that change the way we hear John's call to repent and confess (vv.4-5)?
   - **Leader's note:** In Isaiah, pardon is announced first and the road is built after. Repentance is getting ready for good news, not punishment endured before God arrives. Surface the source's point that honest, specific confession ("confessing their sins," v.5) is the way to mercy (Proverbs 28:13; 1 John 1:9).

3. John was one of the most famous men in the land, yet he said he was not fit even to untie Jesus' sandals (v.7). Why is that kind of humility good news rather than low self-worth?
   - **Leader's note:** John's humility is accurate self-assessment beside Christ: "He must increase, but I must decrease" (John 3:30). He knew he could only give the sign (water). Only Jesus gives the reality (the Spirit, v.8). Our worth is not measured by prominence but by pointing to Christ.

4. God's people have often met him in the wilderness, in seasons stripped of comfort such as illness, grief, or leaving a home. What might it look like to "make ready the way of the Lord" (v.3) in the season you are in right now?
   - **Leader's note:** Draw from the source's Application: the wilderness does not mean God is absent (compare Hosea 2:14, where God speaks tenderly there). It can be a place to simplify, listen, and turn from misplaced trust. Encourage simple steps such as naming a sin to God, thanking him for Jesus, or asking a pastor or friend to walk through it with you. Every believer, whatever their limits, can be a "voice" that points others to Jesus.
