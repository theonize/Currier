# Leader's Notes — James 5:1-6 - Warning to the Rich

## Verses

> **1** Come now, you rich, weep and howl for the miseries that are coming upon you.
>
> **2** Your riches have rotted and your garments are moth-eaten.
>
> **3** Your gold and silver have corroded, and their corrosion will be evidence against you and will eat your flesh like fire. You have laid up treasure in the last days.
>
> **4** Behold, the wages of the laborers who mowed your fields, which you kept back by fraud, are crying out against you, and the cries of the harvesters have reached the ears of the Lord of hosts.
>
> **5** You have lived on the earth in luxury and in self-indulgence. You have fattened your hearts in a day of slaughter.
>
> **6** You have condemned and murdered the righteous person. He does not resist you.
>
> — *English Standard Version (ESV)*

---

## Overview

- **Setting.** Letter from James, half-brother of Jesus and leader of the Jerusalem church, to Jewish believers scattered through the Greek-speaking cities of the Roman East (James 1:1). Probable date c. AD 45–49, possibly the earliest New Testament book. James was himself killed by the Jerusalem aristocracy c. AD 62 (Josephus, *Antiquities* 20.200) — the "righteous one" of v.6 made flesh.
- **Audience in this paragraph.** The "rich" are addressed as if absent — denounced, not counselled, with no call to repent. They are wealthy landowners whose estates ran on day-labourers paid each evening. The real listeners are the believing poor of 2:5 who overhear the oracle and are meant to take comfort: their unpaid wages have already reached heaven.
- **Immediate context.** Second of a pair of "Come now" oracles (4:13; 5:1): last week the presumptuous merchant who forgot God; now the oppressive landlord who defies him. Verse 7 pivots straight back to "brothers... be patient... the Judge is standing at the door" (5:7-9). This paragraph is the verdict that makes that patience reasonable.
- **Genre.** Not wisdom counsel but a **prophetic woe-oracle** in the line of Amos and Isaiah — a diatribe harangue, a mock funeral dirge, a heavenly lawsuit. The rich are summoned to wail their own doom.
- **Literary shape.** A courtroom whose centre is the cry that reaches God:

```
A    Summons to lament -- miseries are coming             (v.1)
  B    Hoarded wealth rots & corrodes -> evidence, fire     (vv.2-3)
    C    Defrauded wages CRY OUT -> ears of the Lord of hosts (v.4)
  B'   Luxury fattens the heart -> for a day of slaughter    (v.5)
A'   Condemned & murdered the righteous one               (v.6)
```

- **Four charges, climbing:** hoarding (property) → defrauding (wages) → self-indulgence (the body) → murder (life). Two witnesses testify: the mute rust (vv.2-3) and the vocal wage (v.4) — two witnesses, as the Law requires.
- **Big idea.** The Lord of hosts hears the cry of the defrauded and has already entered judgment on wealth that is hoarded, stolen, indulged, and weaponized — so the oppressed may wait in patience, and the comfortable must examine what their money is for.
- **Supporting movements.**
  - The rich are summoned not to repent but to howl: the sentence is already passed (v.1).
  - Their hoard has already rotted in heaven's ledger; the corrosion is a courtroom witness that will turn on them like fire (vv.2-3).
  - The withheld wage has a voice, and it has reached the ears of the commander of heaven's armies (v.4).
  - Their luxury is the fattening of cattle for slaughter, and their crushing of the defenceless righteous draws the opposition of the God who "opposes the proud" (vv.5-6).
- **Pastoral guardrails from the source.** James does not condemn wealth as such — Scripture has wealthy saints (Abraham, Job, Joseph of Arimathea). He condemns wealth *hoarded, stolen, indulged, and weaponized*. And the comfort is real: the poor believer is not told to revolt but that the case is already filed in the one court the rich cannot rig.

## Key Verse

> **4** Behold, the wages of the laborers who mowed your fields, which you kept back by fraud, are crying out against you, and the cries of the harvesters have reached the ears of the Lord of hosts.

- **Why this verse:** The source's Literary section places it at the centre of the chiasm and calls it "the theological hinge"; the Linguistic section marks its "Behold!" as the turn of the whole oracle; and the Theological section builds its first timeless principle on it — God hears the cry of the oppressed.

## Info

### 1. Weep and Howl (v.1)

#### v.1
- **"Come now"** (Greek: ἄγε νῦν, *age nyn*) — the same prophet's bark that opened 4:13. The two oracles are a matched pair: the merchant who forgot God, the landlord who defies him.
- **"You rich"** (Greek: πλούσιοι, *plousioi*, G4145) — not the wealthy in general but the exploiting class of 2:6 who "oppress you and drag you into court." Addressed as if absent: a sentence pronounced for the congregation's hearing.
- **"Weep and howl"** — **howl** (Greek: ὀλολύζω, *ololyzō*, G3649) is an onomatopoeic shriek ("ol-ol-ol"; cf. English "ululate"), used nowhere else in the New Testament but everywhere in the Greek Old Testament for the prophets' wail over doomed nations (Isaiah 13:6; Amos 8:3; Joel 1:5).
- Grammar paints it: "break into weeping" (aorist), "wailing continuously" (present participle). Sudden grief, then sustained lament.
- Note what is missing: no call to repent. This is a mock funeral dirge — the rich are handed the keen for their own death (cf. Luke 6:24, "Woe to you who are rich").
- **"Miseries that are coming"** — the verdict is already filed; only the execution is future.
- **Leader's cue:** Establish early who is speaking to whom. James is Amos in a Christian letter, and the room he is really addressing is the believing poor. Ask the group how it would feel to overhear this if you were the unpaid harvester.

### 2. The Hoard That Testifies (vv.2-3)

#### v.2
- Ancient wealth was held in three forms, each with its own nemesis: **produce** (grain, oil, wine) that **rots**; **garments** that the **moth** eats; **gold and silver** that **corrode** (v.3). James names all three so no form of hoarding escapes.
- **"Rotted"** (Greek: σέσηπεν, *sesēpen*, G4595) — a New Testament one-off. **"Moth-eaten"** (Greek: σητόβρωτα, *sētobrōta*, G4598) — literally "moth-devoured," another one-off.
- Fine clothes were real capital then — woven slowly, bequeathed by will, given as royal gifts (Genesis 45:22; 2 Kings 5:5). The moth is their quiet enemy (Job 13:28; Isaiah 51:8).
- The verbs are **perfect tense**: "has rotted," "has become moth-eaten." The rich still see the goods intact; James declares the decay already accomplished — the prophetic perfect.
- Behind it stands Jesus: "Do not lay up treasures on earth, where moth and rust destroy" (Matthew 6:19-20). James turns the Sermon's warning into a verdict.

#### v.3
- **"Corroded"** (Greek: κατίωται, *katiōtai*, G2728) — gold and silver do not actually rust. The impossibility is the point: even "incorruptible" wealth is already under sentence.
- **"Corrosion"** (Greek: ἰός, *ios*, G2447) means both "rust" *and* "venom" — the very word James used for the tongue "full of deadly poison" (3:8). The tarnish on the hoard is poison that turns on its hoarder.
- **"Evidence against you"** (Greek: μαρτύριον, *martyrion*) — courtroom language. The idle hoard takes the witness stand: the fact that it sat unused testifies for the prosecution.
- **"Eat your flesh like fire"** — the decay spreads from the goods to the owner. Fire is the Bible's picture of consuming judgment (Malachi 3:2-3; 4:1).
- **"Laid up treasure"** (Greek: ἐθησαυρίσατε, *ethēsaurisate*, G2343) — the verb of Matthew 6:19. They thought they were storing wealth; they were "storing up wrath" (Romans 2:5).
- **"In the last days"** — the worst possible season to be amassing what decays; the clock has already run out. Cf. the rich fool whose barns were full the night his soul was required (Luke 12:16-21).
- Historical footnote: Jerusalem's Upper City mansions, with their frescoes and imported glass, burned in AD 70; coin hoards buried in the war were never recovered.
- **Leader's cue:** The source is careful — the sin is not having money but what it was *for*, where it *came from*, and whom it *cost*. Ask what "idle abundance" looks like at any stage of life.

### 3. The Wage That Cries Out (v.4)

#### v.4
- **"Behold"** (Greek: ἰδού, *idou*) — the prophet's finger-point, turning the courtroom toward the unheard plaintiffs. Structurally this verse is the centre of the passage.
- **Day-labourers** (Greek: ἐργάται, *ergatai*, G2040) owned no land, were hired each morning in the marketplace (Matthew 20:1-16), and were paid each evening. Without the day's wage a family did not eat that night.
- The Law therefore commanded same-day pay: "you shall give him his wages on the same day, before the sun sets, for he is poor and counts on it... lest he cry against you to the LORD" (Deuteronomy 24:14-15; Leviticus 19:13). Sirach 34:22: "to deprive an employee of wages is to shed blood."
- **"Kept back by fraud"** (Greek: ἀπεστερημένος, *apesterēmenos*, G650) — a manuscript variant reads "withheld / in arrears"; either way it is not a late payment but theft of a man's survival.
- **"Are crying out"** (Greek: κράζει, *krazei*, G2896) — the *wage itself* is the subject of the verb. Unpaid coin is given a voice. Then the harvesters' **cries** (Greek: βοαί, *boai*, G995) join it — two witnesses, the Law's standard of proof (Deuteronomy 19:15).
- This is the Bible's chain of unheard voices: Abel's blood crying from the ground (Genesis 4:10), Israel's groan in Egypt (Exodus 2:23; 3:7), the martyrs' "How long?" (Revelation 6:9-10).
- **"Have reached the ears"** — perfect tense: the cries are *already lodged* in God's hearing, not on their way.
- **"Lord of hosts"** (Greek: κύριος σαβαώθ, *kyrios Sabaōth*, G4519; Hebrew: יְהוָה צְבָאוֹת, *YHWH ṣəḇāʾôṯ*, "LORD of armies") — James leaves the Hebrew untranslated. Used in the New Testament only here and in Romans 9:29 (quoting Isaiah). It is a near-quotation of Isaiah 5:9, the woe on the land-grabbers. Against the landlord's hired hands stands the commander of heaven's armies.
- The geography is vertical: no earthly court will hear the labourer (v.6), so the venue of justice moves upward, to the one jurisdiction the rich cannot capture.
- **Leader's cue:** This is the verse of comfort for the wronged and the verse of dread for the wronger. For this group, lean on the comfort: God is not a neutral observer; every unheard cry is already logged. Surface Psalm 34:17.

### 4. Fattened for Slaughter (vv.5-6)

#### v.5
- **"Luxury"** (Greek: ἐτρυφήσατε, *etryphēsate*, G5171, "lived softly") and **"self-indulgence"** (Greek: ἐσπαταλήσατε, *espatalēsate*, G4684, "lived wantonly") — the second is the word for the pleasure-seeking widow who "is dead even while she lives" (1 Timothy 5:6). Both describe luxury that *softens* a person.
- In their world conspicuous luxury was honour, the currency of status. James reads it as guilt.
- **"Fattened your hearts"** (Greek: ἐθρέψατε, *ethrepsate*, G5142, "fed, fattened") — livestock language. A "fat" heart is a conscience grown gross and insensible (Isaiah 6:10; Psalm 73:7).
- **"In a day of slaughter"** (Greek: ἡμέρᾳ σφαγῆς, *hēmera sphagēs*, G4967) — from Jeremiah 12:3, "set them apart for the day of slaughter." Not "*as* in a day" (a softer manuscript reading) but *in* it: the cattle gorge on the very day they are being fattened *for*. The feast is the abattoir.
- Poetic justice runs through the whole passage: the rich *mow* and *harvest* (v.4), then are themselves *fattened* and *slaughtered*. The reapers become the reaped.
- Luxury's real danger, per the source, is not expense but **numbness** — a life so comfortable it no longer feels the cost of anything, to neighbour or to self.

#### v.6
- **"Condemned"** (Greek: κατεδικάσατε, *katedikasate*, G2613) **"and murdered"** (Greek: ἐφονεύσατε, *ephoneusate*, G5407) — no "and" in the Greek; the two verbs are slammed together. Judicial process and homicide become one act.
- This is **judicial murder**: the rich manipulating courts they control (2:6, "the rich... drag you into court"). Wisdom of Solomon 2:10-20 gives the script: "let us oppress the righteous poor man... condemn him to a shameful death."
- **"The righteous person"** (Greek: τὸν δίκαιον, *ton dikaion*, G1342) — first, the defenceless godly poor. But the language of the condemned Righteous One who does not resist opens onto Isaiah 53:7 ("he opened not his mouth") and onto Christ, "the Righteous One" (Acts 3:14; 7:52; 1 Peter 2:23). Every crushing of the innocent shares in the archetypal crime against the Just One.
- James himself, called "the Just" by the early church, would be judicially killed by the Jerusalem elite c. AD 62.
- **"He does not resist you"** (Greek: οὐκ ἀντιτάσσεται, *ouk antitassetai*, G498) — the hinge wordplay of the letter. It is the exact verb of 4:6: "God **opposes** the proud." The victim does not oppose the rich; the God of the humble does. Some read it as a question ("Does he not oppose you?"); either way the echo is the indictment.
- The oracle ends abruptly on the victim's helplessness — which is precisely the posture James commends to the church in the next breath: "Be patient, therefore, brothers... the Judge is standing at the door" (5:7-9).
- **Leader's cue:** Read 4:6 aloud beside 5:6 so the group hears the same word. Then read 5:7-8 so the passage lands where James lands it: not in revolt, and not in despair, but in patient trust that the Judge has heard.

## Discussion

1. Read v.1 again. James tells the rich to "weep and howl" but never tells them to repent. Who is really meant to hear this speech, and what is it meant to do for them?
   - **Leader's note:** Draw out that the rich are addressed as if absent and that the letter's real readers are the poor believers of 2:5 and the "brothers" of 5:7. The oracle is comfort to the oppressed and warning to the comfortable. Establish that James is speaking as Amos and Isaiah did.
2. In vv.2-3 James says the gold has "corroded" and the corrosion is "evidence against you." Gold does not rust. What is he saying about hoarded wealth, and about what it will one day say about us?
   - **Leader's note:** Surface the prophetic perfect (already rotted in heaven's ledger), the rust-that-is-venom (same word as the tongue's poison, 3:8), and the courtroom image: idle abundance takes the stand. Tie to Matthew 6:19-21 and Luke 12:16-21. Keep the source's guardrail: the sin is not having money but what it was for.
3. Verse 4 says the unpaid wages "are crying out" and have "reached the ears of the Lord of hosts." What does it mean that God hears a cry no human court will hear, and where else in the Bible does that happen?
   - **Leader's note:** Trace the chain: Abel's blood (Genesis 4:10), Israel in Egypt (Exodus 2:23-24), the wage-law (Deuteronomy 24:15), the martyrs under the altar (Revelation 6:9-10). Bring out "Lord of hosts" as the commander of heaven's armies, and the perfect tense — the cry is already lodged. For this group, land it as comfort: no wrong done to you has gone unheard.
4. James says the rich have "fattened their hearts" (v.5) — grown numb through comfort — and that the righteous one "does not resist" (v.6). Where in your own life could comfort be dulling your conscience, and how does trusting the Judge (5:7-9) change how you respond to being wronged?
   - **Leader's note:** Keep it gentle and concrete. From the source's Application: name one thing you are storing that is doing no good for anyone; name one debt of kindness or payment you can settle; pray against numbness. On being wronged, point to 4:6 beside 5:6 and to 1 Peter 2:23 — the righteous entrusts the case to the one who judges justly, and the Judge is at the door.
