# Leader’s Notes — James 5:13-20 - Prayer of faith

## Verses

> **13** Is anyone among you suffering? Let him pray. Is anyone cheerful? Let him sing praise.
>
> **14** Is anyone among you sick? Let him call for the elders of the church, and let them pray over him, anointing him with oil in the name of the Lord.
>
> **15** And the prayer of faith will save the one who is sick, and the Lord will raise him up. And if he has committed sins, he will be forgiven.
>
> **16** Therefore, confess your sins to one another and pray for one another, that you may be healed. The prayer of a righteous person has great power as it is working.
>
> **17** Elijah was a man with a nature like ours, and he prayed fervently that it might not rain, and for three years and six months it did not rain on the earth.
>
> **18** Then he prayed again, and heaven gave rain, and the earth bore its fruit.
>
> **19** My brothers, if anyone among you wanders from the truth and someone brings him back,
>
> **20** let him know that whoever brings back a sinner from his wandering will save his soul from death and will cover a multitude of sins.
>
> — *English Standard Version (ESV)*

---

## Overview

- **Setting:** James writes to “the twelve tribes in the Dispersion” (James 1:1), understood in the research as Jewish believers in Jesus scattered outside Palestine. They were often poor, exposed to injustice, and without medicine or safety nets as we know them. Their congregations were small enough that elders could come to a sickbed and members knew one another well.
- **Author and date:** The research identifies the author as James, the brother of Jesus and leader of the Jerusalem church, writing early (likely c. AD 44-49). Tradition called him “James the Just” and said his knees grew callused “like a camel’s” from constant prayer — a man who practiced what this passage teaches.
- **Immediate context:** James 5:7-12 (last week) called for patient waiting on the Lord. Prayer now becomes the explicit theme. This is the letter’s closing paragraph; it ends with no greeting or benediction, only a charge to bring back the wanderer.
- **Genre and frame:** Pastoral exhortation in a wisdom-shaped letter. Rapid questions answered by commands (vv.13-14), an expansion on prayer’s power (vv.15-16), a proof from Elijah (vv.17-18), and a closing maxim (vv.19-20).
- **Big idea:** In every season of life God’s people turn to him together — praying, praising, confessing, and bringing back the wanderer — because the prayer of a righteous person has great power.
- **Movement 1 — Bring every season to God:** Suffering calls for prayer, gladness for praise, sickness for the elders’ prayer (vv.13-14).
- **Movement 2 — Pray and confess together:** The Lord heals, raises, and forgives; so believers confess sins to one another and pray for one another (vv.15-16).
- **Movement 3 — Pray like Elijah:** An ordinary man prayed fervently, and God shut and opened the heavens (vv.17-18).
- **Movement 4 — Bring back the wanderer:** Turning a straying believer back saves a soul from death (vv.19-20).
- **Place in the letter:** The paragraph bookends James 1: the “prayer of faith” answers asking “in faith” (1:6); “wanders” echoes “do not be deceived” (1:16); “save his soul” echoes “save your souls” (1:21).
- **Leading this group:** Illness is close at hand for many in the room. Hold the source’s caution firmly: James’s promise is a summons to expectant prayer, not a denial of medicine or a guarantee of healing on demand. God sometimes heals the body now and always heals it finally in the resurrection. The “if” of v.15 also means sickness is not automatically a sign of personal sin. Present residents as real intercessors: James insists powerful prayer belongs to ordinary believers.

## Key Verse

> **16** Therefore, confess your sins to one another and pray for one another, that you may be healed. The prayer of a righteous person has great power as it is working.

- **Why this verse:** The Literary Analysis places v.16 at the pivot of the passage’s concentric structure as its thesis, which the Elijah example then proves; the Theological Analysis builds its principles of confession and effective prayer on it.

## Info

### 1. Bring every season to God (vv.13-14)

#### v.13
- Three questions open with “Is anyone among you…?” and scan the whole range of life.
- **Suffering** (Greek: κακοπαθέω, *kakopatheō*) is hardship broadly, including the poverty and injustice of James 5:1-6.
- “Let him pray” is a present command: keep on praying, as a habit.
- **Sing praise** (Greek: ψάλλω, *psallō*) first meant plucking strings, then singing psalms; compare Colossians 3:16.
- Gladness is offered back to God, not spent on self; no condition of life lies outside prayer.
- **Leader cue:** Invite a favorite psalm or hymn that helps in hard days or glad ones.

#### v.14
- **Sick** (Greek: ἀσθενέω, *astheneō*) means “without strength” — illness and weakness.
- The sick person takes the first step: “let him call.” The research names this humility — asking for help rather than suffering silently.
- **Elders** (Greek: πρεσβύτεροι, *presbyteroi*) are the congregation’s recognized leaders, modeled on synagogue elders (Acts 14:23).
- **Anointing** (Greek: ἀλείφω, *aleiphō*) is the everyday word for rubbing on oil, not the ceremonial *chriō*; olive oil was the era’s common medicine (Luke 10:34; Mark 6:13). The source holds this distinction with some caution.
- The grammar makes the prayer, not the oil, the main action.
- “In the name of the Lord” means under his authority — not magic.
- **Leader cue:** Traditions differ on oil; the source treats it as a genuine biblical practice that some retain and others express through laying on of hands. The embodied, prayerful care is what does not change.

### 2. Pray and confess together (vv.15-16)

#### v.15
- “The prayer of faith” answers the doubting asker of James 1:6.
- **Save** (Greek: σῴζω, *sōzō*) means both heal and save; James keeps body and soul together.
- **Raise up** (Greek: ἐγείρω, *egeirō*) is used for leaving a sickbed and for resurrection.
- **Sick** here (Greek: κάμνω, *kamnō*) suggests being worn down or weary.
- “*If* he has committed sins” — sin may be a factor, not always; compare Job and John 9:1-3.
- “Will be forgiven” is a divine passive: God is the one who forgives.
- **Leader cue:** Some here have prayed for healing that has not come. The source says healing is always subordinated to God’s will (James 4:15) and guards against two errors: denying that God heals, and presuming faith can compel him.

#### v.16
- “Therefore” ties mutual confession to the elders’ ministry just described.
- **Confess** (Greek: ἐξομολογέω, *exomologeō*) means to acknowledge openly and fully — “to one another.”
- This does not replace confession to God (1 John 1:9); it expresses reconciled fellowship.
- **Be healed** (Greek: ἰάομαι, *iaomai*) is the word of Isaiah 53:5 in the Greek Old Testament: “by his wounds we are healed.”
- **Has great power as it is working** (Greek: ἐνεργουμένη, *energoumenē*, root of “energy”) — prayer is effective, a real means through which God acts.
- KJV readers will see “confess your *faults*”; the earliest manuscripts read “sins.”
- **Leader cue:** Confession here is with trusted believers, kept confidential, and answered with prayer rather than advice.

### 3. Pray like Elijah (vv.17-18)

#### v.17
- **A nature like ours** (Greek: ὁμοιοπαθής, *homoiopathēs*) — the word Paul and Barnabas used at Lystra: “we also are men, of like nature with you” (Acts 14:15).
- Elijah’s power lay in God who answered, not in a superhuman constitution.
- “Prayed fervently” is literally “with prayer he prayed,” a Hebrew way of stressing earnestness.
- The drought story is 1 Kings 17-18; “three years and six months” matches Luke 4:25.
- God himself directed the drought and the rain (1 Kings 17:1; 18:1): his sovereignty undergirds prayer rather than cancelling it.

#### v.18
- Elijah “prayed again”; heaven gave rain and the earth bore fruit.
- Rain was covenant blessing and drought covenant judgment (Deuteronomy 11:13-17); mercy ended a season of judgment.
- The rain recalls the farmer’s “early and late rains” of v.7, joining patience and prayer.
- The argument runs from lesser to greater: if one frail man’s prayer governed the sky, the church’s prayer of faith is not powerless.
- **Leader cue:** The research calls this “the dignity of frail intercessors.” Prayer offered from a bed or chair is real ministry; invite residents to see themselves as Elijah’s kind.

### 4. Bring back the wanderer (vv.19-20)

#### v.19
- “My brothers” — James closes in the language of family.
- **Wanders** (Greek: πλανάω, *planaō*) pictures straying from a path; it echoes “do not be deceived” (James 1:16).
- “From the truth” recalls “the word of truth” (James 1:18).
- **Brings back** (Greek: ἐπιστρέφω, *epistrephō*) is the Old Testament’s turning/repentance verb (Hebrew: שׁוּב, *shûv*).
- Compare the seeking shepherd (Luke 15:3-7; Matthew 18:12-15) and gentle restoration (Galatians 6:1).

#### v.20
- The soul saved “from death” is best read as the wanderer’s; the death in view is ultimate.
- **Save** (Greek: σῴζω, *sōzō*) returns from v.15: the body there, the soul here.
- “Cover a multitude of sins” draws on Proverbs 10:12 (“love covers all offenses”); compare 1 Peter 4:8.
- Covering means forgiveness, not concealment — “blessed is the one… whose sin is covered” (Psalm 32:1).
- The letter stops here with no benediction, handing the role of restorer to every reader.
- **Leader cue:** The source’s methods are simple — a phone call, a meal, a visit — approached patiently, listening first. A note or a call is within everyone’s reach.

## Discussion

1. What three situations does James name in vv.13-14, and what response does he give for each?
   - **Leader’s note:** Suffering → pray; cheerful → sing praise; sick → call the elders to pray (vv.13-14). Surface that every season of life, hard or glad, is brought to God.

2. Why do you think James joins healing, forgiveness, and confession together in vv.15-16, and what does the small word “if” in v.15 guard against?
   - **Leader’s note:** The God who heals the body also forgives the soul (*sōzō* holds both). The “if” keeps us from assuming every illness comes from personal sin. Handle gently any experience of unanswered prayer for healing.

3. Why does James stress that Elijah was “a man with a nature like ours”? What does that mean for our own prayers?
   - **Leader’s note:** Elijah was ordinary; God answered (v.17). Powerful prayer is not reserved for spiritual celebrities (v.16) — it belongs to every believer, including those whose ministry now happens from a chair or bed.

4. Who could you pray for by name this week — someone sick, someone struggling, or someone who has drifted from the faith — and how might you reach out to them?
   - **Leader’s note:** Draw from the source’s Application: pray first, then a simple act such as a phone call, a note, or a visit, approached patiently and without pressure (vv.19-20).
