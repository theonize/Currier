# Leader's Notes — James 4:13-17 - Boasting About Tomorrow

## Verses

> **13** Come now, you who say, "Today or tomorrow we will go into such and such a town and spend a year there and trade and make a profit"—
>
> **14** yet you do not know what tomorrow will bring. What is your life? For you are a mist that appears for a little time and then vanishes.
>
> **15** Instead you ought to say, "If the Lord wills, we will live and do this or that."
>
> **16** As it is, you boast in your arrogance. All such boasting is evil.
>
> **17** So whoever knows the right thing to do and fails to do it, for him it is sin.
>
> — *English Standard Version (ESV)*

---

## Overview

- **Setting.** Letter from James, half-brother of Jesus and leader of the Jerusalem church, to Jewish believers scattered through the Greek-speaking cities of the Roman world (James 1:1). Probable date c. AD 45–49, possibly the earliest New Testament book. Martyred c. AD 62 (Josephus, *Antiquities* 20.200).
- **Audience in this paragraph.** Not the poor of 2:1-7 or the unpaid labourers of 5:4, but the congregation's traveling wholesale traders — people with capital, mobility, and year-long plans. The Roman peace, roads, sea-lanes, and common currency had made the Mediterranean one trading floor. Their confidence was realistic; that is exactly why it felt so secure.
- **Immediate context.** Follows the rebuke of the one who usurps the seat of "the one lawgiver and judge" (4:11-12). Same disease, new symptom: there, grasping God's right to judge; here, grasping God's command of the future. Opens a twin pair of "Come now" oracles (4:13; 5:1) — the presumptuous merchant and the oppressive rich, the two faces of prosperity that has forgotten God.
- **Genre.** Wisdom exhortation in diatribe form. James quotes an imaginary merchant's own words (v.13), dismantles them (v.14), scripts the words he should have said (v.15), passes verdict (v.16), and closes with a detachable proverb (v.17). The seed text is Proverbs 27:1: "Do not boast about tomorrow, for you do not know what a day may bring." Behind it stands Jesus' parable of the rich fool (Luke 12:16-21).
- **Literary shape.** A frame built around two speeches, pivoting on the mist:

```
A    v.13a  "Come now, you who say"
   B    v.13b  the boast — four futures: go, spend a year, trade, profit
      C    v.14   pivot — you don't know tomorrow; you are a mist
   B'   v.15    the corrected speech — "If the Lord wills..."
A'   v.16    verdict — such boasting is evil
        v.17    coda — knowing good and not doing it is sin
```

- **Big idea.** God alone holds tomorrow, and our life is a vapour, so every plan must be spoken and held under "if the Lord wills" — and once we know that, failing to live it is sin.
- **Supporting movements.**
  - The merchant's plan is not wrong for being a plan; it is wrong for leaving God out of the calendar — a "practical atheism of the daybook."
  - The rebuttal is two facts he edited out: he cannot see one day ahead, and he himself is mist.
  - The cure is not to stop planning but to plan under the Lord's will; the same futures ("we will live and do") now sit under one condition.
  - James then widens the lens: the sin of omission. The good you know and don't do is sin — the letter's "doers, not hearers only" (1:22) turned into a definition.
- **Pastoral guardrails from the source.** Two errors to avoid: turning "Lord willing" into a verbal charm while the heart still grips the plan, and swinging into fatalism that stops planning altogether. James affirms the venture and corrects the swagger.

## Key Verse

> **15** Instead you ought to say, "If the Lord wills, we will live and do this or that."

- **Why this verse:** The source's Hermeneutic section names the hinge of the passage as the contrast between what the merchants *do* say (v.13) and what they *ought* to say (v.15); the Theological section builds its first principle (providence over plans) on it, and every other verse either sets it up or enforces it.

## Main Points

1. **The Plan Without God** (v.13) — James quotes a confident year-long business plan whose only flaw is that God is nowhere in it.
   - **Source:** Historical & Cultural §2, §4, §6; Linguistic §3; Literary §2, §4
2. **You Are a Mist** (v.14) — The planner who schedules a year cannot see one day ahead, because his own life is a vapour that appears and vanishes.
   - **Source:** Linguistic §2, §5, §7; Literary §5; Theological §3; Hermeneutic §1 Thread A
3. **If the Lord Wills** (vv.15-16) — The same plans, spoken under God's will, are right; spoken as self-confident boasting, they are evil.
   - **Source:** Historical & Cultural §4, §7; Theological §1, §4; Hermeneutic §1 Thread C; Application §4
4. **The Good Left Undone** (v.17) — Knowing the right thing and failing to do it is sin — the sin of omission, and the letter's "doers, not hearers" made into a rule.
   - **Source:** Historical & Cultural §4; Linguistic §3, §5; Hermeneutic §1 Thread D; Application §2, §3

## Info

### 1. The Plan Without God (v.13)

#### v.13
- **"Come now"** (Greek: ἄγε νῦν, *age nyn*) — a prophetic buttonhole, found in the New Testament only here and 5:1. A singular command flung at a plural crowd; an attention-grabber, not a greeting.
- James **quotes the merchant's own words** — the diatribe method: let the defendant testify, then cross-examine.
- Four confident futures chained together: **go → spend a year → trade → make a profit**. Place, time, activity, and outcome all mastered in advance. Not one clause for God.
- **"Such and such a town"** — deliberately generic; cities are interchangeable dots on a profit map.
- **"Trade"** (Greek: ἐμπορεύομαι, *emporeuomai*, G1710) — the wholesale traveling merchant (root of "emporium"), not the corner shopkeeper. **"Make a profit"** (Greek: κερδαίνω, *kerdainō*, G2770) — same root as "gain the whole world and forfeit his soul" (Mark 8:36).
- Historically realistic: Roman roads, safe seas, common coinage; traders really did base themselves in a hub city for a sailing season or year. The plural "we" reflects family firms and partnerships, not lone individuals.
- The seed text is **Proverbs 27:1**; the nearest story is the rich fool of Luke 12:16-21 and Sirach 11:18-19 ("I have found rest" — then he dies).
- **Leader's cue:** Stress what James is *not* condemning — planning, travel, commerce, profit. The fault is the missing premise.

### 2. You Are a Mist (v.14)

#### v.14
- **"You do not know what tomorrow will bring"** — the man who schedules a *year* cannot guarantee a *day*. The gap between the two is the measure of his folly.
- **"What is your life?"** — a rhetorical question at the structural centre of the paragraph; the crushing answer comes in the same breath.
- **"Mist"** (Greek: ἀτμίς, *atmis*, G822) — steam off a pot, breath-fog on a cold morning. Rare in the New Testament (here and Acts 2:19); the Greek Old Testament word for the smoke over Sodom (Genesis 19:28) and the vanishing morning mist of Hosea 13:3. It is the Greek echo of Ecclesiastes' *hevel*, "vapour."
- Sound-play in the Greek: the mist **appears** (*phainomenē*) and then **vanishes** (*a-phanizomenē*) — literally "appears" and "un-appears." The word for disappearing is the word for appearing with a negative stuck on the front.
- Text note: older versions (KJV) read "**it** is a vapour"; the better manuscripts read "**you** are a mist." James is not describing their schedule; he is describing *them*.
- Canonical thread: dust animated by God's breath (Genesis 2:7); "my life is a breath" (Job 7:7); "man is like a breath, his days a passing shadow" (Psalm 144:4); "all flesh is grass" (Isaiah 40:6-8; 1 Peter 1:24). Within James: the rich man who fades like a flower (1:10-11).
- Mortality here is not despair but **clarity** — "teach us to number our days" (Psalm 90:12). Mist need not be the last word: the breath God lends, he can raise imperishable (1 Peter 1:24-25).
- **Leader's cue:** The mood shifts here from rebuke to something almost tender. Let the room feel it. For a nursing-home group this verse is not theoretical; handle it as comfort and clarity, not threat.

### 3. If the Lord Wills (vv.15-16)

#### v.15
- **"Instead you ought to say"** — James now scripts the *right* speech. The cure is a corrected sentence, not silence.
- **"If the Lord wills"** (Greek: ἐὰν ὁ κύριος θελήσῃ, *ean ho kyrios thelēsē*) — the *Deo volente* ("D.V.") formula. Paul uses it habitually (Acts 18:21; 1 Corinthians 4:19; 16:7). Jewish piety had the equivalent; even Plato and Epictetus said "if God wills." What James supplies is a single sovereign personal **Lord** instead of fickle Fortune.
- Note what survives: "**we will live and do this or that**." The futures remain; they simply move under one condition. Four self-directed verbs in v.13 become two surrendered ones here.
- **Life itself** ("we will live") is placed first under God's will — before any plan.
- Behind it: "The heart of man plans his way, but the LORD establishes his steps" (Proverbs 16:9; 19:21); "your will be done" (Matthew 6:10); "not my will, but yours" (Luke 22:42).
- Not a verbal charm. The source is emphatic: the words are the method, dependence is the meaning; the phrase recited while the heart still grips the plan is itself the arrogance James condemns.

#### v.16
- **"As it is"** (Greek: νῦν δέ) — a hard "but": their actual conduct set against the humility just prescribed.
- **"You boast in your arrogance"** — **arrogance** (Greek: ἀλαζονεία, *alazoneia*, G212) is the swagger of the *alazōn*, the stock braggart of Greek comedy who claims what he does not have. Here the claim is ownership of tomorrow. Only other New Testament use: "the pride of life" (1 John 2:16).
- Culturally, announcing your plans and projected gains was normal status display. James reframes an honoured habit as **evil** (Greek: πονηρός, *ponēros*) — not merely unwise, because it encroaches on God's prerogative.
- Boasting thread in James: the lowly may boast in exaltation, the rich in humiliation (1:9-10) — boasting rightly aimed; here boasting bent the wrong way.
- **Leader's cue:** Distinguish the *content* of the plan (fine) from the *posture* of the planner (evil). Ask what changes in a sentence when "if the Lord wills" is meant.

### 4. The Good Left Undone (v.17)

#### v.17
- **"So"** (Greek: οὖν) — a "therefore" that turns the specific rebuke into a universal rule; the verse reads like a line lifted from Proverbs.
- **"Whoever knows the right thing to do and fails to do it"** — the **sin of omission**. Sin defined not by breaking a prohibition but by leaving a known duty undone.
- **"Sin"** (Greek: ἁμαρτία, *hamartia*, G266) — "missing the mark." Here the mark missed is the good you saw and did not do.
- In context: now that the right way to plan has been shown, ignoring it is culpable. Knowledge creates obligation (Luke 12:47-48; James 3:1).
- The verb **"do"** (Greek: ποιέω, *poieō*) ties the paragraph together: "spend [make] a year" (v.13), "do this or that" (v.15), "the right thing to do ... fails to do it" (v.17). So much busy doing, and the one needful doing left undone.
- Wider canon: Leviticus 5:1 (guilt for a duty left undone); the servant who knew his master's will (Luke 12:47); the goats condemned for what they did *not* do (Matthew 25:41-45); the priest and Levite who passed by (Luke 10:31-32).
- Within James: "be doers of the word, and not hearers only" (1:22-25); "faith without works is dead" (2:14-17). This verse is the letter's spine hardened into a definition.
- Guardrail: v.17 forbids the opposite error too. Hearing "life is a mist" and ceasing to plan or act is refusing a known good — itself sin.
- **Leader's cue:** Land the application from the source: name the one good you already know to do, identify the real reason for delay, take the smallest first step today, and tell someone who will ask.

## Discussion

1. Read the merchant's speech in v.13 aloud. What exactly does he plan, and who or what is missing from the plan?
   - **Leader's note:** Draw out the four verbs (go, spend a year, trade, profit) and that nothing in the plan is wicked. The only thing missing is God. Establish early that James is not against planning or business.
2. Why does James answer a year-long business plan with a picture of a mist (v.14)? What is he saying about the difference between a year and a day?
   - **Leader's note:** The planner schedules a year but cannot see tomorrow; the "you are a mist" reading makes it personal. Surface Psalm 90:12 — numbering our days is clarity, not gloom. For this group, let it be comfort: our times are in God's hands.
3. What is the difference between saying "If the Lord wills" as a real confession and saying it as a habit? How would you tell the difference in your own speech?
   - **Leader's note:** The plans in v.15 survive; only the premise changes. The test is what happens when the plan collapses — do we respond as people whose times were never ours, or as people robbed? Warn against both the empty phrase and fatalistic passivity.
4. Verse 17 says knowing the good and not doing it is sin. What is one good thing you already know you should do that you have been putting off — and what would the first small step be?
   - **Leader's note:** Keep it concrete and gentle: a call, an apology, a word of thanks, a gift. Offer the source's sequence — name it, name the reason for delay, take the smallest step today, tell someone who will ask.
